\begin{abstract}
Wirsching's 1998 Weak Covering Conjecture asks for a sub-exponential $K(l)$ such that a large
enough set $R_{j-1,j} \subset \Z_3^*$, built from strictly decreasing exponent tuples, is forced to
cover every unit residue modulo $3^l$. We extend the exact computation of $j^*(l)$, the smallest
such covering budget, from $l=20$ to $l=23$, and compare four growth models for
$e(l) := j^*(l) - l\log_4 3$ against the extended table: a constant model is unconditionally
impossible (we prove $e(l)\ge\tfrac14\log_2l-O(1)$) and disfavored at every statistic checked; the
growing alternatives are not distinguished from one another by these criteria. We give
the best bound known to us, $j^*(l) \le \tfrac98 l + \tfrac{33}2$, via a mean-payoff-game relaxation
of the
covering budget whose proof rests on one computationally verified, not generally proven,
correspondence. We then examine the natural Fourier approach to a tighter bound: the natural
full-spectrum positivity criterion is provably unreachable at every budget, since no element of
$R_{j,k}$ is divisible by $3$; granting the criterion regardless, a
uniform square-root-cancellation argument still could not beat a threshold worse than the
mean-payoff bound. Separately, a direct computation at one accessible level finds the bulk of the
primitive-frequency mass sitting outside the sparse exceptional set a fixed magnitude threshold
defines. At the levels checked, the residues
that actually fail to be covered sit in
cells of local hit-density well below the global mean, showing coarse magnitude alone does not
account for which residues resist; a separate phase-randomization experiment at a
different, more tractable pair of
parameters points, descriptively, toward phase structure beyond that. We
prove a theorem on the uncovered residues themselves, for budgets $j\ge l$: the set left uncovered
at a budget is contained in both twice and four times the set left uncovered at the previous budget,
giving an explicit recursive upper bound on $j^*(l)$ that we prove exact at most budgets, given one
further combinatorial property verified for $l=3,\dots,13$ against a table running to $l=23$; at the
one remaining budget, this holds at four small levels checked directly and is otherwise only
empirically exact. We
falsify the natural cost-$1$ form of a local repair rule this theorem might suggest, with explicit
witness-level exhaustive counts, prove that every last-uncovered residue is $\equiv1\pmod3$ whenever
$j^*(l)\ge l+1$ (every computed level but one), prove a two-class containment
law modulo $9$ on the same residues, and report further exact identities, some verified but not
proven, governing the last residues to be covered. Two of the questions this work leaves open are
named and stated as exact conditions, each narrower than the conjecture itself; others are noted
where they arise. The Weak Covering Conjecture remains open.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Collatz map, 3-adic dynamics, covering conjecture, mean-payoff games,
exponential sums, Littlewood--Offord theory.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Wirsching's 1998 monograph~\cite{wirsching1998} studies the density of predecessor sets under the
$3n+1$ map through an averaging construction on the $3$-adic integers. A limiting Markov chain
built from that construction has an invariant density, and Wirsching's own route to positive
predecessor density passes through a covering question, stated in that same
monograph~\cite{wirsching1998}, about a family of finite subsets of $\Z_3^*$, the $3$-adic units. A
later paper of his own isolates this route as a self-contained target and states, as open
conjectures, the gaps still unbridged between it and a proof~\cite{wirsching2003}. For integers
$j,k\ge0$, write
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Rjk}
R_{j,k} := \Bigl\{\, \sum_{i=0}^{j} 2^{a_i}3^i \ \Bigm|\ j+k \ge a_0 > a_1 > \cdots > a_j \ge 0
\,\Bigr\} \subset \Z_3^*.
\end{equation}
Each element of $R_{j,k}$ is built from a strictly decreasing sequence of $j+1$ ``digit positions''
chosen from $\{0,\dots,j+k\}$: the $i$-th chosen position, counting from the top, contributes
$2^{a_i}$ scaled by $3^i$. Wirsching's Weak Covering Conjecture (Conjecture~3.9,
Chapter~V,~\cite{wirsching1998}) asks for a sub-exponential $K(l)$, meaning $\lim_{l\to\infty}
K(l)e^{-\gamma l}=0$ for every constant $\gamma>0$ (Wirsching's own definition, same chapter), such
that $|R_{j-1,j}| \ge K(l)\cdot 3^l$
already forces $R_{j-1,j}$ to cover every unit residue modulo $3^l$; the cardinality on the left is
that of the tuple family itself, before reduction, which by
Proposition~\ref{prop:lowerbound}'s bijection equals $\binom{2j}{j}$; the (a priori much smaller)
size of its image modulo $3^l$ is a different quantity. Writing $j^*(l)$ for the smallest $j$ such
that
$R_{j-1,j}$ covers $(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$ exactly (a quantity that exists for every $l$, unconditionally;
Proposition~\ref{prop:exist} below), the conjecture's forcing form and the growth rate of
$j^*(l)$ coincide once coverage, once achieved, persists at every larger budget: this holds for
$j\ge l$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling}, Section~\ref{sec:holdouts}), which covers every level in
Table~\ref{tab:jstar}, but is not established below $l$, the regime the conjectured rate would
eventually enter. With that proviso, the conjecture concerns the growth rate of
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:el}
e(l) := j^*(l) - l\log_4 3,
\end{equation}
the excess of the true covering budget over the leading-order rate a cardinality argument alone
predicts ($|R_{j-1,j}| = \binom{2j}{j} \sim 4^j/\sqrt{\pi j}$, so matching $2\cdot3^{l-1}$ residues
needs $j \sim l\log_4 3$ at leading order). At the threshold,
$K(l) \asymp |R_{j^*(l)-1,j^*(l)}|/3^l \asymp 4^{e(l)}/\sqrt{j^*(l)}$; the $\sqrt{j^*(l)}$ correction
is polynomial in $l$ at every level actually computed, where $j^*(l)=O(l)$ (Table~\ref{tab:jstar}),
though no unconditional polynomial bound on $j^*(l)$ is proved here (Proposition~\ref{prop:exist}'s
unconditional bound is exponential; Corollary~\ref{cor:mpgbound}'s linear bound is conditional). Since
a polynomial correction never changes whether a sequence is sub-exponential, this identification, like
the rest of this paragraph, carries the persistence proviso above: with that understood, the
conjecture's truth would force $e(l)$ to grow strictly slower than linearly. The converse,
that
$e(l)=o(l)$ would in turn establish the conjecture, needs coverage to persist at every budget past
$j^*(l)$, which Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling} only gives for $j\ge l$; below $l$, the regime a truly
sub-linear $e(l)$ would eventually reach, the converse is open.

An earlier, unpublished manuscript of the author computed $j^*(l)$ exactly for $l=1,\dots,20$ by
direct enumeration and reported the resulting table without a growth-rate analysis. This paper does
three things. First, it extends that table to $l=23$ and gives a proper statistical comparison of
candidate growth models for $e(l)$ against the full range (Sections~\ref{sec:computation}
and~\ref{sec:growth}). Second, it gives the best upper bound on $j^*(l)$ we have found, via a
reformulation of the covering budget as a mean-payoff game, conditional on the empirically verified
correspondence between the two stated in Empirical Result~\ref{er:soundness}
(Section~\ref{sec:mpg}). Third, and at greatest length, it reports a sustained attempt to close the
gap to the conjectured rate directly, first by the naive uniform-cancellation version of the natural
Fourier approach, and, once that version is shown to fail for a checkable reason, by a direct study
of the residues that actually resist coverage (Sections~\ref{sec:gapA}--\ref{sec:further}). That
second attempt does not settle the conjecture. It produces a theorem on those residues, a
falsification of the cost-$1$ form of a repair rule the theorem might suggest, and two named open
questions, each
narrower than the conjecture itself and worth a dedicated attack in its own right.

\section{The computation}
\label{sec:computation}

Table~\ref{tab:jstar} gives $j^*(l)$ and $e(l)$ for $l=1,\dots,23$. The values for $l\le20$
reproduce the earlier manuscript's table exactly; they were independently recomputed here by a
from-scratch Rust reimplementation of the covering search (a bitset rotation dynamic program over
residues modulo $3^l$), validated against the original Python implementation at every $l\le20$
before being trusted for the extension. Each reported $j^*(l)$ is the smallest budget at which the
DP's own coverage bitset is full, so the search certifies both coverage at $j^*(l)$ and its absence
at $j^*(l)-1$ internally, by construction, at every level it runs; the $l\le20$ agreement against an
independently written implementation is this paper's evidence that the search itself is correct; it
is carried forward by inference for $l=21,22,23$, without a separate cross-check at those levels.
The values for $l=21,22,23$ are new.

The search above only tries budgets $j\ge l$ at level $l$, so on its own it certifies $j^*(l)$ only
against smaller candidates within that range.

\begin{lemma}[No smaller budget covers]
\label{lem:nosmaller}
For $l=2,\dots,23$, no $j<l$ has $R_{j-1,j}$ covering $(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$, so $j^*(l)\ge l$ holds outright,
not only relative to the range the search above tries.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $R_{j-1,j}$ covers $(\Z/3^L\Z)^*$ for some $L\ge j$, it covers $(\Z/3^j\Z)^*$ too: reduction
modulo $3^j$ factors through reduction modulo $3^L$, and the ring homomorphism
$\Z/3^L\Z\to\Z/3^j\Z$ restricts to a surjection of unit groups (every unit modulo $3^j$ lifts to a
unit modulo $3^L$), so the image of a full set of units modulo $3^L$ is a full set of units modulo
$3^j$.

$R_{0,1}=\{1,2\}$ has $2$ elements, short of $|(\Z/3^l\Z)^*|=2\cdot3^{l-1}\ge6$ for $l\ge2$, so
$j=1$ never covers at $l\ge2$: $j^*(l)\ge2$ for $l\ge2$.

Fix $l$ with $2\le l\le23$ and suppose, for contradiction, some $j_0$ with $2\le j_0<l$ has
$R_{j_0-1,j_0}$ covering $(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$. The reduction above, with $L=l$, gives $R_{j_0-1,j_0}$
covering $(\Z/3^{j_0}\Z)^*$ too, i.e.\ $j^*(j_0)\le j_0$. But $j_0<l\le23$, so $j_0$ is itself a
level the search above already ran, at budgets starting from $j_0$ itself; Table~\ref{tab:jstar}
reports $j^*(j_0)>j_0$ strictly there. Contradiction. So no $j_0<l$ covers, giving $j^*(l)\ge l$.
\end{proof}

\section{The growth of $e(l)$}
\label{sec:growth}

We compare four candidate functional forms for $e(l)$'s growth against the tail $l=10,\dots,23$
($n=14$; the same range choice as the earlier manuscript, now with three more points), each fit by
ordinary least squares: $e(l)=c$ (constant), $e(l)=a+b\ln l$ (logarithmic), $e(l)=a+b\sqrt l$
(square-root), and $e(l)=a+bl$ (slow-linear). Table~\ref{tab:aic} gives the fit statistics, using the
Gaussian-OLS forms $\mathrm{AIC}=n\ln(\mathrm{RSS}/n)+2k$, $\mathrm{BIC}=n\ln(\mathrm{RSS}/n)+k\ln n$
(RSS the residual sum of squares, $k$ the number of regression coefficients, $1$ for the constant
model and $2$ for the other three), and leave-one-out cross-validated RMSE; since only differences
$\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$, $\Delta\mathrm{BIC}$ are used below, the convention for a shared additive
constant across models does not affect them. $e(l)$ is an exact, deterministic sequence with no
sampling model behind it, so these are read descriptively throughout, as penalized fit scores over
the observed table rather than calibrated statistical evidence.

A constant model is disfavored sharply and consistently: $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=16.09$,
$\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=15.45$, both growing at every $l$ added since $l=21$. The three growth models
are not distinguished from one another by the conventional $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}>2$
rule of thumb, with slow-linear taking a narrow lead on both AIC and leave-one-out cross-validation;
the three candidate regressors ($\log l$, $\sqrt l$, $l$) are themselves highly correlated over this
range (pairwise correlation $\ge0.993$), so this ranking mainly reflects how thin the current margin
is. By the identity noted after~\eqref{eq:el}, a linear $e(l)$ would make $K(l)$ exponential,
falsifying the conjecture outright. Fourteen highly
correlated points cannot distinguish that possibility from logarithmic or square-root growth, so
this comparison does not settle the question either way; it only says which growth rates remain
live.

None of this machinery is actually needed to rule out a constant $e(l)$: since $\log_43<1$, rounding
a constant-plus-linear quantity $l\log_43+c$ to the nearest integer can only change by $0$ or $1$
from one $l$ to the next, yet $j^*(1)=1$ and $j^*(2)=4$, an increment of $3$. A constant-$e(l)$
source is incompatible with the table's first two entries alone, with no statistical test required.

A direct combinatorial fact rules out the same tail even more sharply, restricted to where the model
is not already dead over the full table: the constant-rounding model behind it predicts every
increment
in $\{0,1\}$, which the full table already refutes (the increment of $3$ noted above, and increments
of $2$ elsewhere in the table, e.g.\ $j^*(2)=4$ to $j^*(3)=6$).
Restricted to the $l=10,
\dots,23$ tail the model comparison above uses ($13$ increments over the same $14$ points, all
actually in $\{0,1\}$, consistent with the model's own prediction there), a fixed-$c$ model
$J_l:=\mathrm{round}(l\log_43+c)$ forces the total change over any $13$ consecutive steps into
$\{\lfloor13\log_43\rfloor,\lceil13\log_43\rceil\}=\{10,11\}$, for every $c$ (a standard fact about
$\mathrm{round}$ of an arithmetic progression with irrational slope, checked directly over a fine
grid of $c$). The observed change is $j^*(23)-j^*(10)=27-15=12$, outside that pair for every $c$: no
constant-rounding model can reproduce this tail at all, a conclusion that needs no probability. $e(l)$
is an exact, deterministic
sequence throughout, so nothing in this paragraph carries a sampling interpretation. Among the $13$
increments, exactly one is zero (the $l=15\to16$ plateau noted above); under the constant-rounding
model, $e(l+1)-e(l)$ can only be $1-\log_43\approx0.2075$ or $-\log_43\approx-0.7925$. The plateau
count above and the OLS comparison above are both functions of the same
$14$-point increment sequence, so they are not independent checks, though the plateau observation
bears
specifically on the constant-rounding model, a different object from the free-intercept constant
regression $e(l)=c$ that Table~\ref{tab:aic} fits.

A constant $e(l)$ is disfavored descriptively by every measure above. It is also, unconditionally,
impossible.

\begin{proposition}[Unboundedness of $e(l)$]
\label{prop:lowerbound}
$e(l) \ge \tfrac14\log_2 l - O(1)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Distinct exponent tuples give distinct sums: since the $a_i$ are distinct, $a_{j-1}$ is the unique
minimal one, so $V=\sum_i 2^{a_i}3^i$ has $2$-adic valuation exactly $a_{j-1}$ (every other term is
divisible by a strictly higher power of $2$), recovering $a_{j-1}$ from $V$ directly. The full term
$2^{a_{j-1}}3^{j-1}$ is then subtracted off, and the process iterates on the remaining $j-1$ terms. So $R_{j-1,j}$, in bijection with $j$-subsets of the
$2j$-element set $\{0,\dots,2j-1\}$, has exact cardinality $\binom{2j}{j}$ (this cardinality is
Wirsching's own Corollary~1.8, Chapter~V,~\cite{wirsching1998}; the injectivity argument above is
this paper's).
Covering
$(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$, of size $2\cdot3^{l-1}$, requires the domain to be at least as large as the
codomain: $\binom{2j}{j} \ge 2\cdot3^{l-1}$ is necessary at $j=j^*(l)$ in particular, since
$R_{j^*(l)-1,j^*(l)}$ covers by definition of $j^*(l)$. The explicit bound
$\binom{2n}{n} \le 4^n/\sqrt{3n+1}$, valid for every $n\ge1$, turns this into
$4^j \ge 2\cdot3^{l-1}\sqrt{3j+1}$, hence
\[
j \ge l\log_4 3 - \log_4 3 + \log_4 2 + \tfrac12\log_4(3j+1).
\]
Dropping the last, non-negative term already gives $j \ge l\log_4 3 - O(1)$, so
$3j+1 = \Omega(l)$, hence $\log_4(3j+1) \ge \log_4 l - O(1) = \tfrac12\log_2 l - O(1)$. Feeding
this back into the displayed inequality gives the claimed bound.
\end{proof}

\section{A mean-payoff-game upper bound}
\label{sec:mpg}

\subsection*{The full-precision game}

Fix $l\ge1$. A \emph{full-precision play} from a unit $z_0$ modulo $3^l$ is a sequence of $l$ moves:
at step $i=0,\dots,l-1$, from a unit $z_i$ modulo $3^{l-i}$, a move of cost $d_i\ge0$ is
\emph{legal} when $2^{d_i}z_i\equiv1\pmod3$, and produces $z_{i+1}:=T_{d_i}(z_i):=(2^{d_i+1}z_i-2)/3$,
which is then required to be a unit modulo $3^{l-i-1}$ (automatic at $i=l-1$, where $3^{l-i-1}=1$).
Write $J:=\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}d_i$ for the total cost.

\begin{lemma}[Grounding]
\label{lem:ground}
For a legal full-precision play from $z_0$ with costs $d_0,\dots,d_{l-1}$ and total cost $J$, define
$a_{l-1}:=0$ and $a_{i-1}:=a_i+d_i+1$ for $i=l-1,\dots,1$ (so $a_0=J-d_0+l-1$). Then
\[
2^{J+l-1}z_0 \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{l-1} 2^{a_i}3^i \pmod{3^l}.
\]
The right side is, by construction, an element of $U(l,a_0)$ (Section~\ref{sec:further}) with bottom
exponent $a_{l-1}=0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By induction on the play length $n$ ($n=1,\dots,l$), stated for a play of length $n$ starting at a
unit $z$ modulo $3^n$ (matching the lemma's notation at $n=l$, $z=z_0$). Write
$J_n:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d_i$ and $a_{n-1}:=0$, $a_{i-1}:=a_i+d_i+1$ for $i=n-1,\dots,1$, as above.

Base case $n=1$: the single move has $2^{d_0}z\equiv1\pmod3$, which is exactly
$2^{a_0+d_0}z\equiv2^{a_0}3^0\pmod3$ since $a_0=0$ when $n=1$.

Inductive step: suppose the claim holds at length $n-1$ for the sub-play with costs
$d_1,\dots,d_{n-1}$ starting at $z':=T_{d_0}(z)$, a unit modulo $3^{n-1}$; writing $a'_i:=a_{i+1}$
for this sub-play, $a'_{n-2}=a_{n-1}=0$ matches its base case, and its recursion
$a'_{i-1}=a'_i+d_{i+1}+1$ matches $a_i=a_{i+1}+d_{i+1}+1$ (the original recursion at index $i+1$),
so the induction hypothesis reads
\[
2^{a_1+d_1}z' \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{n-2}2^{a_{i+1}}3^i \pmod{3^{n-1}}.
\]
By definition of $T_{d_0}$, $2^{d_0+1}z\equiv3z'+2\pmod{3^n}$. Multiplying by the unit
$2^{a_1+d_1}$ gives
\[
2^{a_1+d_1+d_0+1}z \equiv 3\cdot2^{a_1+d_1}z' + 2^{a_1+d_1+1} \pmod{3^n}.
\]
Since $a_0=a_1+d_1+1$ (the recursion at $i=1$), the left side is $2^{a_0+d_0}z$ and the last term is
$2^{a_0}$. Multiplying the induction hypothesis by $3$ turns it, modulo $3^n$, into
$3\cdot2^{a_1+d_1}z'\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}2^{a_i}3^i$, so
\[
2^{a_0+d_0}z \equiv 2^{a_0} + \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}2^{a_i}3^i = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{a_i}3^i \pmod{3^n},
\]
the claim at length $n$, using $a_0+d_0=J_n+n-1$ (telescoping the recursion).
\end{proof}

\begin{empresult}[The game's worst case is $j^*(l)$]
\label{er:soundness}
\[
j^*(l) = \max_{z_0}\ \min\{J : \text{$z_0$ has a legal full-precision play of total cost $J$}\},
\]
the maximum over units $z_0$ modulo $3^l$. Verified against the known exact table of $j^*(l)$ for
$l=1,\dots,12$, with no discrepancy; that a legal full-precision play exists at all from every unit
$z_0$ (the minimum inside the display is over a nonempty set) is part of what this verification
confirms empirically. A general proof would additionally need the successor to stay a unit at the
next modulus at each step, a condition the mod-$3$ legality check alone does not decide.
Lemma~\ref{lem:ground} does not by itself establish this: the
twist $2^{J+l-1}$ in the lemma depends on $z_0$ through its own optimal $J$, so it is not a single,
$z_0$-independent relabeling of the unit group, and we do not have a general proof of the displayed
identity.
\end{empresult}

\subsection*{The window-$k$ relaxation}

A move of cost $d\ge0$ at $z$ is \emph{legal} when $2^dz\equiv1\pmod3$, as above, and \emph{safe} at
window $k$ when, for $m:=3^k$, every one of the three residues $z,z+m,z+2m$ modulo $3m$ maps under
$T_d$ back into a unit modulo $m$. A minimizing player, seeing only $z\bmod3^k$, chooses a legal, safe $d$;
an adversary then reveals the hidden next $3$-adic digit $\epsilon\in\{0,1,2\}$, forcing the true
state to $T_d(z+3^k\epsilon)\bmod3^k$. Both requirements matter: legality alone can send a unit to
$0$ (take $z=1$, $d=0$: $T_0(1)=0$), and the safety filter is exactly what keeps the walk inside
$\Z_3^*$ regardless of which digit the adversary supplies. Legality depends on $d$ only through
$d\bmod2$ (since $2$ has order $2$ modulo $3$), and $2$ is a primitive root modulo $3^{k+1}$ for
every $k\ge0$ (order $2\cdot3^k$, by an elementary lifting-the-exponent induction), so
$2^{d+1}\bmod3^{k+1}$, and hence $T_d(z)\bmod3^k$ and the safety check itself, depend on $d$ only
through $d\bmod(2\cdot3^k)$: successor states repeat with this period as $d$ grows, and every
representative beyond the smallest in its residue class has strictly larger cost for the same
effect, so is never used by an optimal policy. The action set at each state is therefore, without
loss, the subset of $\{0,\dots,2\cdot3^k-1\}$ that is legal and safe there, which is finite. That
this subset is nonempty at every unit state has been verified computationally at every $k=3,\dots,14$
used below; a general proof is left open here.

This restricted-visibility game is, by construction, a two-player \emph{mean-payoff game} on the
$2\cdot3^{k-1}$ unit states modulo $3^k$, with the now finite legal, safe moves as the minimizer's
edges, the adversary's three choices of $\epsilon$ as the maximizer's edges, and payoff the long-run
average of the move costs $d$. The classical theory of such games, initiated by Ehrenfeucht and
Mycielski~\cite{ehrenfeuchtmycielski1979}, guarantees a value and an optimal positional policy in
general; Theorem~\ref{thm:mpg} below needs none of that generality, only the twelve specific
policies whose resulting $(\rho_k,C_k)$ are reported in Table~\ref{tab:mpg}, each independently
checked as described in Section~\ref{sec:code}.

A window-$k$ policy, run against the actual digit sequence, gives a legal full-precision play at any
$z$ modulo $3^l$: at step $i$, the true remaining state is $z_i$ modulo $3^{l-i}$, so the policy
sees the true $z_i\bmod3^k$ directly whenever $l-i\ge k$, i.e.\ for every step but the last $k-1$.
Once $l-i<k$, whether because $l<k$ globally or because a play with $l\ge k$ has reached its own
final $k-1$ steps, fix one lift of the true state to a residue modulo $3^k$, at the first such step,
and run the window-$k$ game forward from that lift on the play's own remaining digits (not a fresh
lift at each step), which is exactly the situation safety was built for, with the play's own
remaining digits standing in for the hidden ones, so the chosen move stays legal against the true,
lower-precision state at every step and the successor relation used below holds at every step,
including these last $k-1$. Writing $s_i$ for whichever window-$k$ state $\sigma_k$ sees this way at step
$i$ (the true $z_i\bmod3^k$, or a fixed lift of it), $s_{i+1}$ is by construction the successor
$s_i'(\epsilon)$ under $\sigma_k$'s chosen move and the digit $\epsilon$ that the real play actually
supplies at that step, so $s_0,\dots,s_l$ is exactly a play of the form the proof below sums over,
with move costs equal to the real play's (this regime is part of what the policy-against-the-covering-DP check at
$k=4,7$ against $l$ up to $14$ already exercised, with zero illegal or unsafe moves; every play
there passes through this same final-$k-1$-steps regime regardless of how large $l$ is).

\begin{theorem}[Window relaxation bound, conditional on Empirical Result~\ref{er:soundness}]
\label{thm:mpg}
For each $k=3,\dots,14$, the certified policy underlying Table~\ref{tab:mpg} (a legal, safe move at
every unit state modulo $3^k$, average cost $\rho_k$) gives an explicit rational $C_k$ with
$j^*(l) \le \rho_k\cdot l + C_k$ for all $l$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $k$ in range. The policy $\sigma_k$, the value $\rho_k$, and a potential $h_k:(\Z/3^k\Z)^*\to\Q$
are computed by a nested Howard strategy-improvement solver searching over the finite action set
established above, and $\sigma_k$'s chosen move is checked, state by state and digit by digit,
against
\[
d_{\sigma_k}(s) + h_k(s'(\epsilon)) \le \rho_k + h_k(s)
\]
at every state $s$ and every adversary digit $\epsilon$, where $s'(\epsilon)$ is the successor under
$\sigma_k$'s chosen move. Summing the inequality along any $l$-step play $s_0,\dots,s_l$ telescopes to
\[
\sum_{i=0}^{l-1} d_{\sigma_k}(s_i) \le l\rho_k + h_k(s_0) - h_k(s_l) \le l\rho_k + \bigl(\max h_k -
\min h_k\bigr) =: l\rho_k + C_k,
\]
regardless of which digits the adversary actually supplies, since the potential inequality holds for
every $\epsilon$ at every step. So $\sigma_k$, run as above, gives every unit $z$ modulo $3^l$ a
legal full-precision play of total cost at most $l\rho_k+C_k$; the minimum cost over all legal plays
from $z$ is therefore also at most $l\rho_k+C_k$, hence so is the worst case over $z$. Only the
$j^*(l)\le\max_{z_0}\min\{J\}$ direction of Empirical Result~\ref{er:soundness}'s equality is used
here, to identify that worst case with $j^*(l)$; the reverse direction plays no role in this proof.
\end{proof}

$\sigma_k$, $h_k$ and $\rho_k$ are computed as rationals by a nested Howard strategy-improvement
solver, which searches actions $d$ up to a fixed cap of $40$ (well above the largest $d$ any
certified policy below actually uses at any $k=3,\dots,14$, which is $11$). The potential inequality
above is checked only for $\sigma_k$'s chosen move at each state, against all three adversary digits,
never against the rest of the action set; that chosen move's own legality and safety are verified
directly from the full-game definitions above ($2^dz\equiv1\pmod3$ and the three-lift condition),
which do not reference the cap at all, so the resulting upper bound $j^*(l)\le\rho_kl+C_k$ does not
depend on it either. The solver
also extracts a matching adversary lower bound from the same run, but that computation ranges only
over the same capped action set: ``upper bound equals lower bound'' certifies $\rho_k$ exactly as the
value of the mean-payoff game restricted to $d\le40$, not, without further argument, as the value of
the unrestricted game on the full action set $\{0,\dots,2\cdot3^k-1\}$ described above, since a move
with $d>40$, unavailable to the capped lower-bound computation, could in principle let the
minimizing player achieve a lower true value than the capped certificate rules out. Table~\ref{tab:mpg}
reports $\rho_k$, $C_k$ under this understanding: certified rates of an exhibited, verified policy,
which is all Theorem~\ref{thm:mpg} needs, not a proven-exact value of the full window-$k$ game.
$\sigma_k$ and $\rho_k$ are cross-validated by an independent value-iteration solver and, at $k=4$ and
$k=7$, by running the resulting policy against the actual finite covering DP for every unit residue
modulo $3^l$, $l$ up to $14$, with zero illegal or unsafe moves encountered.
Table~\ref{tab:mpg} gives the values computed for $k=3,\dots,14$.

\begin{corollary}[Conditional on Empirical Result~\ref{er:soundness}]
\label{cor:mpgbound}
$j^*(l) \le \tfrac98 l + \tfrac{33}2$.
\end{corollary}

This is, to our knowledge, the best bound on $j^*(l)$'s growth rate currently available, conditional
on Empirical Result~\ref{er:soundness}. $\rho_k$ is observed to be non-increasing over the computed
range $k=3,\dots,14$ (Table~\ref{tab:mpg}); we have not verified this in general, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:mpgbound} uses only the single value $k=14$. The general technique, representing a
covering-type combinatorial problem as a mean-payoff game and computing exact rational relaxation
bounds this way, is not new to this application: beyond Ehrenfeucht and Mycielski's foundational
theorem~\cite{ehrenfeuchtmycielski1979} itself, a close and recent methodological precedent proves
rationality and computability of the covering radius of a primitive sofic shift by essentially the
same construction, applied to a different object~\cite{meyerovitchyoung2026}. We cite both as
precedent for the framing; what is new here is its specific application to $j^*(l)$.

\begin{remark}
Fitting $\rho_k = L + A/k$ by ordinary least squares to $k=10,\dots,14$ gives $L\approx0.876$,
still well above the conjectured true rate $\log_4 3 = 0.7925\ldots$; the fit is sensitive to the
range used (all of $k=3,\dots,14$ gives $L\approx0.904$) and is not evidence of any specific limit.
Only the direction $j^*(l)\le\rho_k l + C_k$, proven above, is established. The reverse inequality,
$\inf_k \rho_k \le \limsup_l j^*(l)/l$, which would be needed to conclude anything about where
$\rho_k$ is heading, is not established.
\end{remark}

\section{The exponential sum, and why it resists a direct attack}
\label{sec:gapA}

Corollary~\ref{cor:mpgbound}'s conditional bound, $1.125\,l$, is well above the conjectured rate
$\log_4(3)\,l \approx 0.7925\,l$. The natural route to closing that gap is Fourier-analytic. Here
$e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}$ denotes the standard additive character, unrelated to the sequence $e(l)$ of
\eqref{eq:el}. By orthogonality on $\Z/3^l\Z$, the number of tuples landing in
residue class $z$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Nz}
N(z) = \frac1{3^l}\sum_{t=0}^{3^l-1} S(t)\,e\!\left(-\frac{tz}{3^l}\right), \qquad
S(t) := \sum_{j+k\ge a_0>\cdots>a_j\ge0} e\!\left(\frac{t}{3^l}\sum_{i=0}^j 2^{a_i}3^i\right),
\end{equation}
and $S(0)=|R_{j,k}|=:T$. The natural route to positivity is the triangle inequality: $N(z)>0$ for
every $z$ as soon as $\sum_{t\ne0}|S(t)| < T$. This route is closed before it opens.
Proposition~\ref{prop:sqrtceiling} below shows the sum on the left is never below $T$, for any
$l$, $j$, $k$: the criterion cannot be reached at all, for any budget. The remainder
of this section studies what is left once the full-spectrum route is abandoned: a primitive-frequency
version of the same idea (\S5.2), and the further reasons below why it resists too.

\subsection{A hard ceiling on uniform cancellation}

\begin{proposition}[The full-spectrum criterion is unreachable]
\label{prop:sqrtceiling}
For every $l\ge1$ and every $R_{j,k}$ with $T:=|R_{j,k}|>0$, $\sum_{t\ne0}|S(t)| \ge T$; in
particular $|S(3^{l-1})|\ge T/2$. Consequently the criterion $\sum_{t\ne0}|S(t)|<T$ is never
satisfied, for any $l$, $j$, $k$, and neither is the uniform bound $|S(t)|=o(T/3^l)$ at every
nonzero $t$, since that would force $\sum_{t\ne0}|S(t)|=o(T)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every element of $R_{j,k}$ reduces to $2^{a_0}\bmod3$ (all other terms carry a positive power of
$3$), so it is $\equiv1$ or $2\pmod3$: no element is divisible by $3$. Hence $N(z)=0$ for every $z$
divisible by $3$, in particular $N(0)=0$, so $\sum_{t=0}^{3^l-1}S(t)=3^lN(0)=0$ and
$\sum_{t\ne0}S(t)=-S(0)=-T$; the triangle inequality gives $\sum_{t\ne0}|S(t)|\ge\bigl|\sum_{t\ne0}
S(t)\bigr|=T$.

The same collapse localizes: summing the inversion formula over the $3^{l-1}$ residues divisible by
$3$ leaves only the three frequencies $t\in\{0,3^{l-1},2\cdot3^{l-1}\}$ in the inner sum (every other
character is nonconstant on that coset of the index-$3$ subgroup and averages to $0$), giving
$S(3^{l-1})+S(2\cdot3^{l-1})=-T$ exactly, at every $l$, $j$, $k$. Since $S(3^l-t)=\overline{S(t)}$,
the two terms are conjugate, so $\mathrm{Re}\,S(3^{l-1})=-T/2$ exactly and $|S(3^{l-1})|\ge T/2$: a
single conjugate pair already forces the bound, before the rest of the spectrum is added in.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:sqrtceiling} closes the naive route outright, at every $j$: there is no
threshold past which it starts to work. Even granting the unreachable premise for
comparison, specializing $S(t)$, $T$ to the family $R_{j-1,j}$ this paper's covering question
concerns ($T=\binom{2j}{j}$) and asking what $j$ a uniform bound $|S(t)|\le C\sqrt{T}$
(square-root cancellation), applied via the triangle inequality across all $3^l-1$ nonzero
frequencies, would force gives $(3^l-1)C\sqrt{T} < T$, so $T > ((3^l-1)C)^2$, i.e.\
$4^j/\sqrt{3j+1} \ge T > ((3^l-1)C)^2$ (the explicit bound from Proposition~\ref{prop:lowerbound}'s
proof), hence $j \ge 2\log_4(3)\,l - O(1) \approx 1.585\,l - O(1)$: already \emph{worse} than
Corollary~\ref{cor:mpgbound}'s $1.125\,l$, on top of resting on a premise
Proposition~\ref{prop:sqrtceiling} rules out at every $j$. Both facts together rule out the naive
version of the Fourier approach, and direct attention to whether a finer, frequency-dependent
treatment (a major/minor-arc split, in the language of the circle method) can do better.

\subsection{A computation: the exceptional mass does not stay small}

Numerically, $|S(t)|$ is far from uniform. Writing $t=3^{l-c}t'$ with $3\nmid t'$ (valuation
$l-c$), $t\cdot V/3^l=t'\cdot V/3^c$ for every tuple value $V$, so $S(t)$ at modulus $3^l$ equals the
same sum evaluated at the coarser modulus $3^c$: a frequency of valuation $l-c$ is literally the
level-$c$ sum of the same tuple family. Proposition~\ref{prop:sqrtceiling}'s exact bound
$|S(3^{l-1})|\ge T/2$ is only a lower bound, no claim of maximality: at $m=1$, $l=2$ (the family
$R_{0,1}=\{1,2\}$, $T=2$), $|S(3)|=1$ but $|S(1)|=2\cos(\pi/9)\approx1.879$ exceeds it, so the top
pair is not always the largest frequency. At the levels directly checked in the family this section
actually uses ($R_{m-1,m}$
at each level's own first-order cardinality threshold: $l=10,m=9$;
$l=12,m=11$; $l=14,m=13$), the top pair $t=3^{l-1},2\cdot3^{l-1}$ is observed to be the largest, and
high valuation correlates with large magnitude beyond it, but loosely: among the twelve
largest frequencies at $l=10$, valuations run $9,9,8,8,7,7,8,8,8,8,7,7$, and at $l=12$ the largest
primitive (valuation-$0$) frequency already exceeds the weakest valuation-$8$ frequency checked. The
natural
repair is to restrict to primitive frequencies, excising the whole non-primitive spectrum (every
valuation $c<l$, the pair above included) and controlling the remainder by an averaged bound;
whether that remainder stays
sparse enough at the scale that matters is a question this section answers computationally, for the family $R_{j-1,j}$ at $l=18$,
$j=16$ (the first-order cardinality threshold, $T:=\binom{32}{16}$, six budgets below the actual
covering threshold $j^*(18)=22$: coverage necessarily fails here already, and this level sits below
the $j\ge l$ range Sections~\ref{sec:holdouts}--\ref{sec:further} otherwise work in), with a negative
result. Write
$\|S\|_1:=\sum_{\gcd(t,3)=1}|S(t)|/T$ for the normalized $L^1$ mass over frequencies coprime to $3$.
This restriction excludes $t=3^{l-1}$, whose magnitude $|S(3^{l-1})|\sim T/\sqrt3$ is fixed by
Proposition~\ref{prop:1547} and, empirically, is the largest among frequencies checked at accessible
$l$, along with its conjugate $t=2\cdot3^{l-1}$ (since
$S(3^l-t)=\overline{S(t)}$, that frequency matches it exactly and is excluded for the same reason).
The restriction to $\gcd(t,3)=1$ also excludes every nonprimitive frequency, roughly a third of the
spectrum, entirely, so $\|S\|_1$ below measures only the primitive-frequency mass. Here
$\|S\|_1=5226.01$. A fixed threshold makes the visible spikes look sparse, but of the $8{,}014$
primitive frequencies with $|S(t)|/T$ exceeding $0.001$, their combined contribution to $\|S\|_1$ is
about $12.2$, so over $99.7\%$ of the primitive-frequency mass at this single computed level lives in
an exponentially numerous population of coefficients individually below the threshold, with no claim
here about how that mass is distributed within it. This is evidence
against a sparse-exceptional-set repair among primitive frequencies at this accessible level; it
says nothing about nonprimitive frequencies, about every $l$, and, since $j=16$ sits six budgets
below $j^*(18)$, still less about the mass distribution near the actual covering threshold.

The same $2^a3^b$-type structure connects the problem to a 2011 discussion of Terence
Tao's, applying Littlewood--Offord and Riesz-product methods to an object built from the same
$2^a3^b$-type exponential sums, in the closely related setting of a hypothetical non-trivial
Collatz cycle~\cite{tao2011blog}; his construction is structurally close to a boundary slice of
$R_{j,k}$ with the bottom exponent pinned at $0$. His own conclusion, in his own words, is that
``once one uses the transcendence theory bound, \dots{} Littlewood-Offord techniques are no longer
of much use,'' and that he knows ``of no way to get a nontrivial separation property between powers
of $2$ and powers of $3$ other than via transcendence theory methods.''

\subsection{A direct test of the residues that actually fail}

The most direct test examines the residues that actually fail to be covered, one budget step
before covering succeeds, i.e.\ $H(l,j^*(l)-1)$ (Section~\ref{sec:holdouts}). For $l=10,\dots,15$
this set has size $2,1,3,1,3,1$ respectively, tiny against a global occupancy mean
$\lambda=|R_{j^*(l)-2,j^*(l)-1}|/(2\cdot3^{l-1})$ that is itself in the thousands over the same
levels (from $1019$ at $l=10$ to $3695$ at $l=15$). Write the \emph{depth-$c$ cell} of a residue $z$
for its class modulo $3^c$, and its \emph{depth-$c$ local intensity} $\lambda_c(z)$ for the total
hit count within that class divided by $3^{l-c}$, the class's size (defined for $c<l$; at $c=l$ the
class is $\{z\}$ itself, and $\lambda_l(z)=0$ identically for any holdout, a degenerate case excluded
below). A \emph{non-homogeneous Poisson model} fit to that intensity assigns $z$ hole probability
$e^{-\lambda_c(z)}$. Computed directly from the exact histogram for $l=10,\dots,15$, every one of the
eleven holdouts across these six levels has $\lambda_c$ well below the global mean at every depth
$c=8,9,10$ with $c<l$ (at $l=10$, only $c=8,9$; $\lambda_c$ from $2.0$ to $108.4$, hole probability
from $e^{-2}$ to $e^{-108}$). The non-homogeneous Poisson model assigns probability $e^{-108}$ to an
event that occurred; $z$, the levels, and the depths checked are all selected after seeing the
holdouts, so this is not a predeclared, calibrated test: the exponent is suggestive rather than a
formal
rejection, and the model is not treated as a live candidate below on that basis. What survives is
the weaker, descriptive claim
underneath it:
holdouts sit systematically in low-intensity cells, with how far below the mean varying widely by
depth and level. A separate
diagnostic at a smaller, more tractable
pair, $(l,m)=(14,16)$, for the family $R_{m-1,m}$ (mean hit count $188.5$, three budgets below the
covering threshold $j^*(14)=19$), holds every $|S(t)|$ fixed and randomizes the phases of
conjugate-symmetric pairs. Writing $N_l(z)$ for the exact hit count of residue $z$ and $N_{l-1}$ for
the same tuple family's count one level down, restricting to primitive frequencies $3\nmid t$ gives
exactly $\sum_{3\nmid t}S(t)e(-tz/3^l) = 3^{l-1}\bigl(3N_l(z)-N_{l-1}(z\bmod3^{l-1})\bigr)$, a
measure of imbalance among the three lifts of each parent residue, identically $0$ at every
non-unit $z$ (both $N_l(z)$ and $N_{l-1}(z\bmod3^{l-1})$ vanish there, since no element of a
tuple family is divisible by $3$). Over all $z$, the actual ratio
of this quantity's maximum absolute value to its root-mean-square is $15.79$, while eight
independent phase scrambles (fixed seed)
give ratios in $[5.11,5.24]$ (a second, independently seeded batch of eight gives the wider
$[4.98,5.40]$, so eight trials do not pin the null down tightly). The scrambles hold every $|S(t)|$
fixed but do not preserve the
identically-zero-off-units constraint just derived, or integrality, or the level-$l$-to-level-$(l-1)$
consistency the real histograms satisfy; that constraint alone accounts for a factor of exactly
$\sqrt{3/2}\approx1.22$ of the gap between $15.79$ and either scramble range (the ratio between an
all-$z$ and a units-only root-mean-square once a third of the terms are forced to $0$), leaving a
residual factor near $2.5$ that this identity alone does not explain. Eight
trials are too few to calibrate a rigorous significance level regardless (a rank-based test against
eight draws
cannot reach below $p=1/9$, however extreme the observed value); what the residual factor suggests,
descriptively, is that the actual phases are not fully exchangeable with a generic-phase null
respecting
only $|S(t)|$, beyond what the vanishing constraint alone forces, though this diagnostic is silent on
what mechanism produces that residual, on
whether it is what
decides coverage, or on how much of it a properly calibrated test would confirm. At the levels
checked, the two diagnostics are not evidence of the same thing: the raw holdout rarity shows that
coarse local intensity (a cell-averaged count) does not by itself pick out which residues resist,
while only the phase-scramble gap bears on phase as such, and even there only descriptively, for the
reasons just given.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:1547}
$|S(3^{l-1})|/\binom{2m}{m} \to 1/\sqrt3$ as $m\to\infty$, for the family $R_{m-1,m}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$\sum_i 2^{a_i}3^i \equiv 2^{a_0} \pmod 3$, so $S(3^{l-1})/\binom{2m}{m}$ depends only on the
limiting distribution of the top exponent $a_0$'s parity as $m\to\infty$, which converges to the
mixture $\tfrac13\omega + \tfrac23\omega^2$ of the two nontrivial cube roots of unity
$\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$ (the limiting gap $2m-1-a_0$ of the maximum of a random $m$-subset of
$\{0,\dots,2m-1\}$ is geometric with parameter $1/2$, giving $\Pr[a_0\text{ even}]\to1/3$). This
mixture equals $-\tfrac12 - \tfrac{i\sqrt3}6$, of modulus $1/\sqrt3$.
\end{proof}

$t/3^l=1/3$ exactly, so $l$ drops out of $S(3^{l-1})$ entirely (only $V\bmod3$ matters), and the
limit above holds at every scale $m$, including along the covering-relevant diagonal $m\sim
l\log_4(3)$. It sharpens Proposition~\ref{prop:sqrtceiling}'s exact bound $|S(3^{l-1})|\ge T/2$ to
the exact asymptotic ratio $1/\sqrt3\approx0.577$, and confirms that the mass Proposition
\ref{prop:sqrtceiling} forces concentrates, at this one frequency at least, exactly where the
numerics of \S5.2 place it: at the highest-valuation frequency, $t/3^l=1/3$.

\section{A theorem on the residues that resist coverage}
\label{sec:holdouts}

Write $H(l,j)$ for the set of units modulo $3^l$ \emph{not} covered by $R_{j-1,j}$. By definition of
$j^*(l)$ as the least covering budget, $H(l,j^*(l))=\emptyset$ and $H(l,j)\ne\emptyset$ for every
$j<j^*(l)$; whether this extends to ``$j\ge j^*(l)$ exactly when $H(l,j)=\emptyset$'' needs emptiness
to persist at every larger budget too, which is not automatic from the definition alone. Every result
in this section needs $j\ge l$; $j^*(l) \ge l$ holds outright for $l=1,\dots,23$ (equality only at $l=1$),
by Lemma~\ref{lem:nosmaller} above, but the conjectured rate $\log_4 3 < 1$ would eventually put
$j^*(l)$ below $l$, outside this section's reach. Within this range, persistence does hold:
Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling}
below gives $H(l,j)=\emptyset\Rightarrow H(l,j+1)\subseteq2\emptyset\cap4\emptyset=\emptyset$, so
``$H(l,j)=\emptyset$ exactly when $j\ge j^*(l)$'' is established for every $j\ge l$. We make no claim
about $j<l$.

\begin{theorem}[Holdout doubling inclusion]
\label{thm:doubling}
For $j \ge l$, $H(l,j+1) \subseteq 2H(l,j) \cap 4H(l,j)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$R_{j-1,j}$ is the set of values $r=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}2^{b_i}3^i$ over $j$-subsets
$\{b_0>\cdots>b_{j-1}\}\subseteq\{0,\dots,2j-1\}$, and $R_{j,j+1}$ the same with $(j{+}1)$-subsets of
$\{0,\dots,2j+1\}$. Fix $r\in R_{j-1,j}$ with exponent set $S$, and $s\in\{1,2\}$. Shifting every
exponent up by $s$ gives $S+s\subseteq\{s,\dots,2j-1+s\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,2j+1\}$, a $j$-subset
whose induced value is $2^sr$ (a uniform shift of exponents preserves relative rank, so each term's
power of $3$ is unchanged). Since $S+s$ contains no $0$, adjoining $0$ as the new smallest exponent
gives a $(j{+}1)$-subset of $\{0,\dots,2j+1\}$: it becomes the new rank-$j$ term, contributing
$2^0\cdot3^j=3^j$. So this witness represents $2^sr+3^j$, and $3^j\equiv0\pmod{3^l}$ once $j\ge l$.
Hence $2R_{j-1,j}\cup4R_{j-1,j}\subseteq R_{j,j+1}\pmod{3^l}$ for $j\ge l$. So if
$z\in H(l,j+1)$, i.e.\ $z$ is not represented at budget $j+1$, then $z$ lies in neither
$2R_{j-1,j}$ nor $4R_{j-1,j}$: both $z/2$ and $z/4$ are unrepresented at budget $j$, i.e.\
$z\in2H(l,j)\cap4H(l,j)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Chain contraction]
\label{cor:chain}
For $j\ge l$: if $x, 2x, \dots, 2^{t-1}x \in H(l,j+1)$ are pairwise distinct (all reduced modulo
$3^l$, $t\ge1$), then $2^ix \in H(l,j)$ for every $i=-2,\dots,t-2$ (i.e.\ $x/4,x/2$ and, when $t\ge2$,
$x,2x,\dots,2^{t-2}x$), pairwise distinct, a chain of length $t+1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling} to each of the $t$ elements: $2^ix\in H(l,j+1)$ gives
$2^{i-1}x, 2^{i-2}x\in H(l,j)$ for $i=0,\dots,t-1$. The union of these pairs over all $i$ is exactly
$\{2^ix : i=-2,\dots,t-2\}$, $t+1$ elements indexed by $t+1$ consecutive powers of $2$. Since $2$
has order $2\cdot3^{l-1}$ modulo $3^l$ (an elementary lifting-the-exponent fact, used already in
Section~\ref{sec:mpg}), any $2\cdot3^{l-1}$ consecutive powers of $2$ times $x$ exhaust every unit
modulo $3^l$, and any shorter run is pairwise distinct. The hypothesis that $x,2x,\dots,2^{t-1}x$ are
pairwise distinct already forces $t\le2\cdot3^{l-1}$, so it remains to rule out equality. If
$t=2\cdot3^{l-1}$, those $t$ elements would be every unit modulo $3^l$, including every element of
the (nonempty) image of $R_{j,j+1}$; but $H(l,j+1)$, the complement of that image, contains no such
element. Hence $t<2\cdot3^{l-1}$, and the $t+1$ output elements, a shorter run of consecutive powers,
are pairwise distinct.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Run-length bootstrap]
\label{cor:bootstrap}
Writing $\mathrm{maxrun}(H)$ for the greatest $t$ such that $H$ contains $t$ pairwise distinct
elements of the form $x,2x,\dots,2^{t-1}x$ (all reduced modulo $3^l$), with
$\mathrm{maxrun}(\emptyset):=0$, $j^*(l) \le j + \mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))$ for every $j\ge l$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $r:=\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))$ and suppose, for contradiction, $H(l,j+r)\ne\emptyset$; pick
$z\in H(l,j+r)$, a chain of length $1$ ($t=1$) in the sense of Corollary~\ref{cor:chain}. Applying
Corollary~\ref{cor:chain} once at each of the $r$ budgets $j+r-1,j+r-2,\dots,j$ (all $\ge l$, since
$j\ge l$), a chain of length $t$ at budget $j'+1$ becomes a chain of length $t+1$ at budget $j'$
each time, so after $r$ applications the length-$1$ chain at $j+r$ becomes a chain of length $r+1$
in $H(l,j)$, contradicting $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))=r$. So $H(l,j+r)=\emptyset$, i.e.\
$j^*(l)\le j+r=j+\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))$.
\end{proof}

\begin{empresult}
\label{er:bootstrap}
Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap} is empirically exact, $j^*(l) = j + \mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))$, at every
$(l,j)$ with $l+1\le j\le j^*(l)$ we were able to compute, $l=5,\dots,21$ at every such budget and
$l=22$ at the single budget $j=l+1$, including a case with $|H(l,j)| > 3\times10^6$
($l=3,4$ were not separately computed here, but Proposition~\ref{prop:tight} below proves the same
equality there). (Past
$j^*(l)$, $H(l,j)=\emptyset$ and $\mathrm{maxrun}(\emptyset)=0$, so the displayed equality no longer
holds there; the claim is only about budgets up to and including $j^*(l)$.) The lower endpoint
$j=l+1$ has a real justification behind it: equality at $j=l$ itself holds for $l=1,\dots,5$ but
fails from $l=6$ on (e.g.\ $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(6,6))=5$, giving $j+\mathrm{maxrun}=11\ne10=j^*(6)$;
$\mathrm{maxrun}(H(7,7))=5$, giving $12\ne11=j^*(7)$); Corollary~\ref{cor:chain} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap} both need $j\ge l$ to apply at all, but that alone does not explain why
$j=l$ specifically starts failing at $l=6$. The sub-range $l+2\le j\le j^*(l)$ is proven at
$l=3,\dots,13$: Proposition~\ref{prop:tight} below, which also proves the boundary
budget $j=l+1$ at $l=3,4,5,6$ specifically. From $l=7$ on, $j=l+1$ remains empirical only, tied to
Proposition~\ref{prop:h014}'s own open converse.
\end{empresult}

A stronger, per-witness converse of Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling}, that $x/2,x/4\in H(l,j)$ already
forces $x\in H(l,j+1)$, which would in turn make a maximal doubling chain both sufficient and
necessary for survival to a given budget, is false as an unrestricted statement:
$1547 \in 2H(7,8)\cap4H(7,8)$, yet $1547 \notin H(7,9)$ (both of $1547$'s two
budget-$9$ witnesses span the full exponent range, the one case Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling}'s
argument does not directly control). Section~\ref{sec:further} returns to a restricted, weaker form
of this converse (corner-redundancy, not the per-witness statement this counterexample refutes) that
remains open in general and is proven at specific levels.

A natural strengthening of Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling}, that every individual witness admits a
bounded-cost local repair, is also false in its most optimistic form.

Call the \emph{Durfee depth} of a witness $\{a_0>\cdots>a_{j-1}\}\in R_{j-1,j}$ at budget $j$ the
count $\#\{i : a_i \ge j\}$, the number of chosen exponents at or above the budget itself.

\begin{empresult}[Failure of the cost-$1$ local repair rule]
\label{er:falsify}
At the budget transition $(l,j)=(6,10)\to(7,11)$, call a unit modulo $3^7$ a \emph{child} and its
reduction modulo $3^6$ its \emph{parent}; $100$ of the $1458$ children (all units modulo $3^7$) have
minimum achievable Durfee depth exceeding their parent's minimum depth by more than one, up to
three; at $(7,11)\to(8,12)$, $332$ of $4374$ children (all units modulo $3^8$) do, likewise up to
three. A correspondingly exhaustive check defines the \emph{repair cost} of a child residue $r$ with
parent residue $p$ as $\min_{S,S'}|S\triangle S'|$, the smallest symmetric difference of exponent
sets over every witness $S\in R_{j-1,j}$ of $p$ and every witness $S'\in R_{j,j+1}$ of $r$ ($|S|=j$,
$|S'|=j+1$, so this distance is always odd, $\ge1$, and the minimum ranges over the full witness
fiber at each budget). This finds repair costs up to $7$ at both
transitions, against a cost of $1$ for the overwhelming majority. This is evidence against a repair
cost of exactly $1$ at these two transitions; it does not establish that no bounded repair cost
exists.
\end{empresult}

This falsifies, in its originally proposed cost-$1$ form, a natural conjecture this project had
earlier entertained (that coverage propagates via a uniform, cost-$1$ local repair rule budget by
budget); its valid surviving content is Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling} and its corollaries above.

\section{Further structure of the last holdouts}
\label{sec:further}

The residues in $H(l,j^*(l)-1)$, the last to be covered, satisfy several further exact laws. For
$W\ge l-1$, write
\[
U(l,W) := \Bigl\{\, \textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{l-1} 2^{\beta_i}3^i \bmod 3^l \ \Bigm|\
W\ge\beta_0>\cdots>\beta_{l-1}\ge0 \,\Bigr\},
\]
an $l$-term analogue of $R_{j-1,j}$ built from a fixed term count and a growing exponent ceiling
$W$.

\begin{lemma}[Width scaling identity]
\label{lem:UW}
For $j\ge l\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:UW}
R_{j-1,j} \equiv 2^{j-l}\,U(l,j+l-1) \pmod{3^l}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $3^i\equiv0\pmod{3^l}$ for $i\ge l$, the residue of $r=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}2^{b_i}3^i\in R_{j-1,j}$
modulo $3^l$ depends only on its top $l$ exponents $b_0>\cdots>b_{l-1}$. A decreasing $l$-tuple
$b_0>\cdots>b_{l-1}\ge0$ with $b_0\le2j-1$ extends to a full admissible $j$-tuple in
$\{0,\dots,2j-1\}$ (appending $j-l$ further, strictly smaller, distinct exponents) exactly when
$b_{l-1}\ge j-l$: append $b_{l-1}-1,\dots,b_{l-1}-(j-l)$ when this holds, and no extension exists
when it does not, since fewer than $j-l$ nonnegative integers lie below $b_{l-1}$. Writing
$c_i := b_i-(j-l)$ turns $b_{l-1}\ge j-l$ into $c_{l-1}\ge0$ and $b_0\le2j-1$ into
$c_0\le2j-1-(j-l)=j+l-1$, and $\sum_i2^{b_i}3^i = 2^{j-l}\sum_i2^{c_i}3^i$. So the top-$l$-exponent
tuples realizable by $R_{j-1,j}$ are exactly $2^{j-l}$ times the decreasing $l$-tuples counted by
$U(l,j+l-1)$, giving the claimed identity modulo $3^l$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Existence]
\label{prop:exist}
$j^*(l)$ exists for every $l\ge1$, with $j^*(l)\le2\cdot3^{l-1}-1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$U(l,\cdot)$ is monotone increasing in $W$: enlarging the range $\{0,\dots,W\}$ only adds admissible
exponent tuples. Shifting every exponent of a tuple realizing $u\in U(l,W)$ up by $1$ gives a
decreasing $l$-tuple with top exponent at most $W+1$ and bottom exponent at least $1>0$, still
admissible, realizing $2u$; hence $2\,U(l,W)\subseteq U(l,W+1)$ for every $W\ge l-1$. The tuple
$\beta_i=l-1-i$ ($i=0,\dots,l-1$) is admissible at $W=l-1$ and gives
$u_0:=\sum_i2^{l-1-i}3^i\equiv2^{l-1}\pmod3$, a unit, so $u_0\in U(l,l-1)$. Iterating the inclusion,
$2^ku_0\in U(l,l-1+k)$ for every $k\ge0$. Since $2$ has order $2\cdot3^{l-1}$ modulo $3^l$ (already
used in Section~\ref{sec:mpg}), $\{2^ku_0 : 0\le k<2\cdot3^{l-1}\}$ is the coset $u_0\langle2\rangle
=(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$: every unit modulo $3^l$ appears as $2^ku_0$ for some $k$ in that range, hence lies in
$U(l,l-1+k)\subseteq U(l,W^\dagger)$, $W^\dagger:=l+2\cdot3^{l-1}-2$, by monotonicity. So
$U(l,W^\dagger)=(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:UW} at $j=W^\dagger-l+1=2\cdot3^{l-1}-1\ge l$ gives
$R_{j-1,j}\equiv(\Z/3^l\Z)^*\pmod{3^l}$, hence $j^*(l)\le2\cdot3^{l-1}-1$.
\end{proof}

This bound is exponential, far above the conjectured $\log_43\cdot l$ rate; it establishes only that
$j^*(l)$ is a well-defined finite quantity, which the rest of this section and
Section~\ref{sec:mpg}'s mean-payoff-game argument then bound far more tightly.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:UW}, which itself needs $j\ge l$, the smallest $j\ge l$ with
$U(l,j+l-1)=(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$ is the smallest $j\ge l$ at which $R_{j-1,j}$ covers; since $j^*(l)\ge l$
for $l=1,\dots,23$, with equality only at $l=1$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:nosmaller}), this coincides with
$j^*(l)$ itself throughout the range this paper works in, though nothing here rules out coverage at
some unchecked $j<l$ for a level not yet computed. Writing $W^*(l):=j^*(l)+l-2$ for the width one
step before that,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:HviaK}
H(l,j) = 2^{j-l}\,D(l,j+l-1), \qquad D(l,W) := (\Z/3^l\Z)^* \setminus U(l,W).
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Last-holdout parity]
\label{thm:parity}
If $j^*(l)\ge l+1$, every element of $H(l,j^*(l)-1)$ is $\equiv1\pmod3$. This covers $l=2,\dots,23$
(Table~\ref{tab:jstar}); the hypothesis is inapplicable only at $l=1$ ($j^*(1)=1$, outside
\eqref{eq:HviaK}'s range), which says nothing about whether the conclusion itself would hold there,
and would become inapplicable again for any $l$ at which the conjectured rate $\log_43<1$ eventually
pushes $j^*(l)$ below $l+1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$U(l,\cdot)$ is monotone increasing in $W$, so $D(l,\cdot)$ is monotone decreasing; every
$x\in D(l,W^*(l))$ is, by minimality of $j^*(l)$, representable at width $W^*(l)+1$ but not at
$W^*(l)$. Any witness for $x$ at width $W^*(l)+1$ using only exponents $\le W^*(l)$ would already
witness $x$ at that smaller width, so every such witness has top exponent
$\beta_0 = W^*(l)+1$. Its contribution to the value, at rank $0$, is $2^{\beta_0}\cdot3^0 =
2^{W^*(l)+1}$, so $x \equiv 2^{W^*(l)+1} \equiv (-1)^{W^*(l)+1} \pmod3$. By \eqref{eq:HviaK}, which
needs $j^*(l)-1\ge l$, elements of $H(l,j^*(l)-1)$ are $2^{j^*(l)-1-l}x$ for such $x$, hence
\[
2^{j^*(l)-1-l}x \equiv (-1)^{j^*(l)-1-l}\cdot(-1)^{W^*(l)+1} = (-1)^{2j^*(l)-2} = 1 \pmod3,
\]
using $W^*(l)+1 = j^*(l)+l-1$ and that $2j^*(l)-2$ is always even.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Near-extinction: forward containment]
\label{prop:extinctionfwd}
If $j^*(l)\ge l+2$, $H(l,j^*(l)-1) \subseteq 2\cdot\{x \in H(l,j^*(l)-2) : x\equiv2\!\!\pmod3\}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $J:=j^*(l)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling} at $j'=J-2\ge l$ gives $H(l,J-1)\subseteq2H(l,J-2)$, so
every $y\in H(l,J-1)$ equals $2x$ for a (unique, since $2$ is a unit) $x\in H(l,J-2)$. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:parity}, $y\equiv1\pmod3$; since $y=2x$, $2x\equiv1\pmod3$, i.e.\ $x\equiv2\pmod3$.
So $x$ lies in $\{x\in H(l,J-2):x\equiv2\pmod3\}$ and $y=2x$ lies in twice that set.
\end{proof}

\begin{empresult}[Near-extinction bijection]
\label{er:extinction}
$H(l,j^*(l)-1) = 2\cdot\{x \in H(l,j^*(l)-2) : x\equiv2\!\!\pmod3\}$, exactly, verified at every
$l=2,\dots,9$ against the exact table with no exception. Proposition~\ref{prop:extinctionfwd} proves
the $\subseteq$ direction unconditionally (at every level with $j^*(l)\ge l+2$, true throughout this
range); only the reverse containment, that every $x\equiv2\pmod3$ in $H(l,j^*(l)-2)$ actually
survives via $2x$, remains empirical.
\end{empresult}

The same width mechanism that proves Theorem~\ref{thm:parity} governs the reverse containment one
step earlier, through the second-highest exponent $\beta_1$ of the extremal witness; unlike the
top-exponent argument above, pinning $\beta_1$ down needs the one-step width identity of
Lemma~\ref{lem:width} below. We have not carried that argument through to a full proof of this
direction and record it as empirically verified only.

A natural cross-level congruence relating $H(l+1,j^*(l+1)-1)$ to $H(l,j^*(l)-1)$ does not hold.
Writing $\pi_l$ for reduction modulo $3^l$, the candidate congruence $x\equiv4\,\pi_l(x)\pmod{3^{l+1}}$
is impossible whenever $\pi_l(x)$ is a unit and $l\ge2$: reducing both sides modulo $3^l$ forces
$\pi_l(x)\equiv4\,\pi_l(x)\pmod{3^l}$, i.e.\ $3\,\pi_l(x)\equiv0\pmod{3^l}$. Checked exhaustively at
$l=2,\dots,6$, $\pi_l(x)$ never lies in $H(l,j^*(l)-1)$ for $x\in H(l+1,j^*(l+1)-1)$ in the first
place, so the pattern has no witnessing instance at these levels either. No cross-level relation
between $H(l+1,\cdot)$ and $H(l,\cdot)$ is established by this paper.

\begin{proposition}[Mod-$9$ two-class containment]
\label{prop:mod9}
For $l\ge2$, writing $J:=j^*(l)$, if $J\ge l+1$ then $H(l,J-1) \bmod 9 \subseteq \{4^J,
4^{J+1}\}\pmod9$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
As in Theorem~\ref{thm:parity}'s proof, every witness of $x\in D(l,W^*(l))$ at width $W^*(l)+1$ has
top exponent $\beta_0=W^*(l)+1$; modulo $9$, only the rank-$0$ and rank-$1$ terms survive (rank
$i\ge2$ contributes a multiple of $3^2$), so $x\equiv2^{\beta_0}+3\cdot2^{\beta_1}\pmod9$ for the
witness's second-highest exponent $\beta_1$. By \eqref{eq:HviaK}, elements of $H(l,J-1)$ are
$y=2^{J-1-l}x$, so $y\equiv2^{J-1-l+\beta_0}+3\cdot2^{J-1-l+\beta_1}\pmod9$; using
$\beta_0=W^*(l)+1=J+l-1$, the first term is $2^{2J-2}=4^{J-1}\bmod9$, fixed. The second term,
$3\cdot2^m\pmod9$ for $m:=J-1-l+\beta_1$, depends only on the parity of $m$ (since $2^m\bmod3$ has
period $2$), taking exactly the two values $3$ or $6\pmod9$ regardless of $\beta_1$'s actual value;
a direct check gives $\{4^{J-1}+3,4^{J-1}+6\}\equiv\{4^J,4^{J+1}\}\pmod9$ for every $J$. So
$y\bmod9\in\{4^J,4^{J+1}\}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{empresult}[Exclusion of the lower class]
\label{er:mod9}
The class actually occupied is $4^{J+1}$, never $4^J$, at every level checked, $l=2,\dots,16$.
Excluding $4^J$ in general is the
sharpest concrete open question this part of the investigation produced: Proposition~\ref{prop:mod9}
places no constraint on $\beta_1$ itself, only on the parity-dependent outcome, so ruling out one of
the two parities needs a different argument.
\end{empresult}

\begin{proposition}[Sufficiency of bounded maxrun]
\label{prop:h014}
If $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,l+1))$ is bounded by a constant $C$ for all $l$, then
$j^*(l) \le l + C + 1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap} at $j=l+1$.
\end{proof}

The converse is not established, and the natural argument for it does not work: reading
Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap} downward gives only $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,l+1)) \ge j^*(l)-l-1$, a
\emph{lower} bound on maxrun, which does not force maxrun to stay bounded when $j^*(l)-l$ does.
Proving the converse would need an upper bound on maxrun in terms of $j^*(l)-l$, equivalent to a
tightness statement for Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap} that this section's own corner-redundancy
question below addresses and that is false in the strong, per-witness form: recall
$1547$ from Section~\ref{sec:holdouts}.

A naive expectation, that a random set of holdouts at the observed density would have doubling
chains far longer than the flat ceiling of $3$--$4$ measured at every level $l=5,\dots,22$, does not
survive contact with the actual, rapidly declining density of $H(l,l+1)$. An independence model
matched to that actual density tracks the observed sequence within about one unit at every level,
including both the one rise and the one fall in it: $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,l+1))$ is typical for the
set's density under that model. This comparison stays descriptive rather than a specified statistical
test (no
distributional family, wraparound convention, or reference statistic is fixed here), and does not,
by itself, say anything about whether
$\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,l+1))$ is bounded;
Proposition~\ref{prop:h014} is the only established link between that question and $j^*(l)$'s rate.

\begin{lemma}[One-step width identity]
\label{lem:width}
Let $\mathrm{Corner}(l,W+1)$ be the set of values realized by a witness tuple
$\beta_0>\cdots>\beta_{l-1}$ with $\beta_0=W+1$ and $\beta_{l-1}=0$ simultaneously. Then
\[
U(l,W+1) = U(l,W) \cup 2U(l,W) \cup \mathrm{Corner}(l,W+1).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\supseteq$: $U(l,W)\subseteq U(l,W+1)$ by monotonicity, $2U(l,W)\subseteq U(l,W+1)$ by the shift
argument of Proposition~\ref{prop:exist}'s proof, and $\mathrm{Corner}(l,W+1)\subseteq U(l,W+1)$
since a corner witness is, in particular, a witness at width $W+1$.

$\subseteq$: let $u\in U(l,W+1)$, witnessed by $W+1\ge\beta_0>\cdots>\beta_{l-1}\ge0$. If
$\beta_0\le W$, the tuple already witnesses $u$ at width $W$, so $u\in U(l,W)$. If $\beta_0=W+1$ and
$\beta_{l-1}\ge1$, subtracting $1$ from every exponent gives a decreasing tuple in $[0,W]$
(top exponent $W$, bottom exponent $\ge0$) witnessing $u/2$, so $u\in2U(l,W)$. If $\beta_0=W+1$ and
$\beta_{l-1}=0$, this is exactly the corner case, so $u\in\mathrm{Corner}(l,W+1)$. These three cases
are exhaustive.
\end{proof}

At every width $W$ in the range $2l+1\le W\le j^*(l)+l-1$,
checked exhaustively for $l=3,\dots,13$ (the upper end is the largest width the exact table reaches
at each level), $\mathrm{Corner}(l,W+1)$ contributes nothing new: every corner witness's value is
already covered without it. Call this the \emph{corner-redundancy} property at width $W$; whether it
holds at every width $W\ge2l+1$ in general, beyond the levels checked, is open. It also holds,
checked directly, at the one smaller width $W=2l$ itself for $l=3,4,5,6$; from $l=7$ through $13$ it
is checked to fail there. The same exhaustive check also covers every width strictly below $2l$, down
to $W=l-1$, at every $l=3,\dots,13$, though no result in this paper depends on that wider range.
$W=2l$ is exactly $j=l+1$,
Proposition~\ref{prop:h014}'s own
case; corner-redundancy at that width is known, one way or the other, at every level $l=3,\dots,13$,
holding only for $l=3,4,5,6$. What remains open is Proposition~\ref{prop:h014}'s own converse itself:
corner-redundancy's failure at $W=2l$ for $l\ge7$ does not settle it either way, it only means this
particular mechanism does not prove it there.

\begin{proposition}[Corner-redundancy implies tightness]
\label{prop:tight}
Fix $j$ with $l+2\le j\le j^*(l)$. If corner-redundancy holds at every width $2l+1\le W\le
j^*(l)+l-1$, then $j^*(l)=j+\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))$ exactly.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Corner-redundancy at width $W$ says $\mathrm{Corner}(l,W+1)\subseteq U(l,W)\cup2U(l,W)$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:width} gives $U(l,W+1)=U(l,W)\cup2U(l,W)$. Multiplication by $2$ is a bijection of
$(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$, so it commutes with complementation and with intersection; taking complements turns
this into $D(l,W+1)=D(l,W)\cap2D(l,W)$, and pushing through the shift in \eqref{eq:HviaK} (itself a
bijection, for the same reason) turns the width-level equality at $W=j'+l-1$ into an equality of
holdout sets, $H(l,j'+1)=2H(l,j')\cap4H(l,j')$, sharpening Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling}'s inclusion to
an identity whenever corner-redundancy holds at that width.

Claim: for every $j'$ with $j\le j'<j^*(l)$, $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j'+1))=\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j'))-1$,
given corner-redundancy at width $j'+l-1$. Since $j'<j^*(l)$, minimality of $j^*(l)$ gives
$H(l,j')\ne\emptyset$, so $s:=\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j'))\ge1$ throughout. The inequality $\le$ is
unconditional: if $H(l,j'+1)=\emptyset$ it reads $0\le s-1$, true since $s\ge1$; otherwise a maximal
chain of length $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j'+1))\ge1$ in $H(l,j'+1)$ extends, by Corollary~\ref{cor:chain},
to a chain one longer in $H(l,j')$. For $\ge$, fix a maximal chain $x,2x,\dots,2^{s-1}x\in H(l,j')$.
For each $k=2,\dots,s$, both $2^{k-1}x$ and $2^{k-2}x$ lie in this chain, so the equality
$H(l,j'+1)=2H(l,j')\cap4H(l,j')$ places $2^kx\in H(l,j'+1)$; this gives the $s-1$ consecutive powers
$4x,8x,\dots,2^sx\in H(l,j'+1)$, so $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j'+1))\ge s-1$, proving the claim.

The widths this uses, $j'+l-1$ for $j\le j'<j^*(l)$, all lie in $[j+l-1,j^*(l)+l-2]\subseteq[2l+1,
j^*(l)+l-1]$ (using $j\ge l+2$), inside the hypothesis, regardless of $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))$'s
actual value. Telescoping the claim over every step from $j'=j$ to $j'=j^*(l)-1$ (a fixed number of
steps, $j^*(l)-j$, fixed before any of this) gives
$\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j^*(l)))=\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))-(j^*(l)-j)$. The left side is $0$
($H(l,j^*(l))=\emptyset$ by definition), so $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))=j^*(l)-j$, i.e.\
$j^*(l)=j+\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))$.
\end{proof}

Since corner-redundancy is verified at every width the proof above uses for $l=3,\dots,13$,
Proposition~\ref{prop:tight} is unconditional at those levels: Empirical Result~\ref{er:bootstrap}'s
equality is a proven theorem there, for every $j$ with $l+2\le j\le j^*(l)$. In general, whether this
implication's hypothesis holds is exactly the corner-redundancy question above.

At $l=3,4,5,6$, where corner-redundancy also holds at the boundary width $W=2l$, the same argument
(starting the telescoping at $j'=l+1$ instead of $j'\ge l+2$) proves the boundary case too:
$j^*(l)=(l+1)+\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,l+1))$ exactly, verified directly ($l=3$: $4+2=6$; $l=4$: $5+2=7$;
$l=5$: $6+3=9$; $l=6$: $7+3=10$). This is the per-level identity behind
Proposition~\ref{prop:h014}'s own converse, proven at these four levels specifically; the converse
itself is a claim about every $l$, needing the identity at every level, these four included. At
$l=7,\dots,13$, corner-redundancy at $W=2l$ is checked to fail, so
this mechanism does not reach the converse there; beyond $l=13$, corner-redundancy at $W=2l$ is
itself unchecked. The converse remains open at every $l\ge7$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Two named questions summarize what stands between this paper's results and a sharper theorem.
Excluding the lower class in Proposition~\ref{prop:mod9}'s containment (Empirical
Result~\ref{er:mod9}) would turn the observed single-class pattern into a fully proven statement.
Proving corner-redundancy at every width $W\ge2l+1$ would, by Proposition~\ref{prop:tight}, make
Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap}'s bootstrap tight for every budget $l+2\le j\le j^*(l)$ in general, not
merely at the levels $l=3,\dots,13$ where corner-redundancy is already verified; it would not, by
itself, settle Proposition~\ref{prop:h014}'s converse at every level, which turns on the boundary
budget $j=l+1$ ($W=2l$), established above only at $l=3,4,5,6$, where corner-redundancy holds there
too; at $l=7,\dots,13$, where it is checked to fail there, the converse remains open by this
mechanism, and beyond $l=13$ both the property and the converse are open. Neither question is, on
its face, as hard as the Weak Covering Conjecture itself; whether either is much easier is not known.

A finite exponential sum built from strictly decreasing, exponentially weighted digit tuples is a
natural object beyond this specific covering problem: finite-population $L$-statistics at non-CLT
frequencies and weights, Littlewood--Offord theory for structured coefficient sequences, and the
analytic side of the Collatz literature via Tao's discussion are all adjacent areas. Findings here
may recur there under different names. The full-spectrum, uniform-cancellation route this paper tries
is unreachable outright (Proposition~\ref{prop:sqrtceiling}); stronger, frequency-dependent
cancellation strategies remain untried. Exceptional mass does
not concentrate on a sparse set at the one accessible scale checked ($l=18$, $j=16$, one threshold,
primitive frequencies only). Holdout rarity shows coarse magnitude alone does not account for which
residues resist, and a separate phase-randomization diagnostic suggests, descriptively, a departure
from a generic-phase model at the levels checked, though neither calibrates that suggestion or says
whether it is what decides coverage. Tao's later, unrelated
result
that almost every Collatz orbit attains an almost bounded value~\cite{tao2019} works with a
first-passage random variable over a different probabilistic model of the same map and does not
bear on covering directly, but is the clearest instance of a new kind of technique changing what
counts as tractable in this area, the kind of shift a resolution of either open question above would
need. Predecessor-density bounds on the $3n+1$ orbit structure itself, most developed in Krasikov
and Lagarias's difference-inequality approach~\cite{krasikovlagarias2003} (a lower bound of
$x^{0.84}$ on the count of integers below $x$ whose orbit reaches $1$), target the same broad
question as Wirsching's own reduction from covering to positive predecessor density, by a different
mechanism: orbit-level difference inequalities rather than the covering structure of one
finite family $R_{j-1,j}$. Nothing in that literature currently transfers to $K(l)$ or $j^*(l)$
directly, but the shared target makes a transfer more plausible than an unrelated result would, and
whether one exists is itself an open question.

The Weak Covering Conjecture itself, and the exact asymptotic rate of $e(l)$, remain open.
