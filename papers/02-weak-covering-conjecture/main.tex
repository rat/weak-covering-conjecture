\begin{abstract}
Wirsching's 1998 Weak Covering Conjecture asks for a sub-exponential $K(l)$ such that a large
enough set $R_{j-1,j} \subset \Z_3^*$, built from strictly decreasing exponent tuples, is forced to
cover every unit residue modulo $3^l$. We extend the exact computation of $j^*(l)$, the smallest
such covering budget, from $l=20$ to $l=23$, and compare four growth models for
$e(l) := j^*(l) - l\log_4 3$ against the extended table: a constant model is disfavored at every
statistic checked, but logarithmic, square-root, and slow-linear growth remain statistically
indistinguishable from one another. We give the best bound known to us,
$j^*(l) \le \tfrac98 l + O(1)$, via a mean-payoff-game relaxation of the covering budget whose
proof rests on one computationally verified, not generally proven, correspondence. We then
examine the natural Fourier approach to a tighter bound: a uniform square-root-cancellation
argument is capped below even this bound, a direct computation at one accessible level finds the
bulk of the relevant exponential sum's mass sitting outside any sparse exceptional set, and the
residues that actually fail to be covered sit at extremal cells that a phase-randomization
experiment shows are decided by phase structure that magnitude alone does not capture. We prove a theorem on the
uncovered residues themselves: the set left uncovered at one budget is contained in the union of
its double and its quadruple at the next, giving an explicit recursive upper bound on $j^*(l)$ that
is empirically exact at every budget checked. We falsify the natural cost-$1$ form of a local
repair rule this theorem might suggest, with explicit witness-level exhaustive counts, prove that
every last-uncovered residue is $\equiv1\pmod3$, and report several further exact identities,
verified but not proven, governing the last residues to be covered. Two precisely stated open
questions remain, each narrower than the conjecture itself. The Weak Covering Conjecture remains
open.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Collatz map, 3-adic dynamics, covering conjecture, mean-payoff games,
exponential sums, Littlewood--Offord theory.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Wirsching's 1998 monograph~\cite{wirsching1998} studies the density of predecessor sets under the
$3n+1$ map through an averaging construction on the $3$-adic integers. A limiting Markov chain
built from that construction has an invariant density, and Wirsching's own route to positive
predecessor density passes through a covering question about a family of finite subsets of
$\Z_3^*$, the $3$-adic units. For integers $j,k\ge0$, write
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Rjk}
R_{j,k} := \Bigl\{\, \sum_{i=0}^{j} 2^{a_i}3^i \ \Bigm|\ j+k \ge a_0 > a_1 > \cdots > a_j \ge 0
\,\Bigr\} \subset \Z_3^*.
\end{equation}
Each element of $R_{j,k}$ is built from a strictly decreasing sequence of $j+1$ ``digit positions''
chosen from $\{0,\dots,j+k\}$: the $i$-th chosen position, counting from the top, contributes
$2^{a_i}$ scaled by $3^i$. Wirsching's Weak Covering Conjecture (Conjecture~3.9,
Chapter~V,~\cite{wirsching1998}) asks for a sub-exponential $K(l)$ such that $|R_{j-1,j}| \ge K(l)\cdot 3^l$
already forces $R_{j-1,j}$ to cover every unit residue modulo $3^l$; the cardinality on the left is
that of the tuple family itself, before reduction, which by
Proposition~\ref{prop:lowerbound}'s bijection equals $\binom{2j}{j}$, not the (a priori much
smaller) size of its image modulo $3^l$. Equivalently, writing $j^*(l)$ for the smallest $j$ such
that $R_{j-1,j}$ covers $(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$ exactly, the conjecture concerns the growth rate of
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:el}
e(l) := j^*(l) - l\log_4 3,
\end{equation}
the excess of the true covering budget over the leading-order rate a cardinality argument alone
predicts ($|R_{j-1,j}| = \binom{2j}{j} \sim 4^j$, so matching $2\cdot3^{l-1}$ residues needs
$j \sim l\log_4 3$ at leading order).

An earlier, unpublished manuscript of the author computed $j^*(l)$ exactly for $l=1,\dots,20$ by
direct enumeration and reported the resulting table without a growth-rate analysis. This paper does
three things. First, it extends that table to $l=23$ and gives a proper statistical comparison of
candidate growth models for $e(l)$ against the full range (Sections~\ref{sec:computation}
and~\ref{sec:growth}). Second, it gives the best upper bound on $j^*(l)$ we have found, via a
reformulation of the covering budget as a mean-payoff game
(Section~\ref{sec:mpg}). Third, and at greatest length, it reports a sustained attempt to close the
gap to the conjectured rate directly, first by the natural Fourier approach, and, once that approach
is shown to fail for a precise and checkable reason, by a direct study of the residues that actually
resist coverage (Sections~\ref{sec:gapA} and~\ref{sec:holdouts}). That second attempt does not
settle the conjecture. It produces a theorem on those residues, a falsification of the cost-$1$
form of a repair rule the theorem might suggest, and two precisely named open questions, each
narrower than the conjecture itself and worth a dedicated attack in its own right.

\section{The growth of $e(l)$}
\label{sec:growth}

We compare four candidate functional forms for $e(l)$'s growth against the tail $l=10,\dots,23$
($n=14$; the same range choice as the earlier manuscript, now with three more points), each fit by
ordinary least squares: $e(l)=c$ (constant), $e(l)=a+b\ln l$ (logarithmic), $e(l)=a+b\sqrt l$
(square-root), and $e(l)=a+bl$ (slow-linear). Table~\ref{tab:aic} gives the fit statistics.

A constant model is disfavored sharply and consistently: $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=16.09$,
$\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=15.45$, both growing at every $l$ added since $l=21$. The three growth models
remain statistically indistinguishable from one another by the conventional $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}>2$
rule of thumb, with slow-linear taking a narrow lead on both AIC and leave-one-out cross-validation;
the three candidate regressors ($\log l$, $\sqrt l$, $l$) are themselves highly correlated over this
range (pairwise correlation $\ge0.993$), so this ranking says more about how thin the margin
currently is than about which functional form is correct.

A direct combinatorial test tells the same story from a different angle. Among the $22$ increments
$j^*(l+1)-j^*(l)$ for $l=1,\dots,22$, exactly one is zero (the $l=15\to16$ plateau noted above). The
earlier manuscript's own table, $l\le20$, showed $3.9$ plateaus expected in $19$ increments under a
constant-$e(l)$ rounding model; taking $q=3.9/19\approx0.2053$ as that model's plateau probability
and testing the observed count against $\mathrm{Bin}(22,q)$, one-sided, gives $p=0.0426$, crossing
the conventional $0.05$ threshold for the first time with the $l=23$ point included; restricted to
the same tail the model comparison above uses ($n=13$), the same test gives $p\approx0.22$. We
report both figures: $e(l)$ is an exact, deterministic sequence, so neither AIC/BIC nor this test
carries the sampling interpretation of a classical inference over noisy data. Both are best read
descriptively, as ``how well does a constant-$e(l)$ source fit the observed increment pattern.''

A constant $e(l)$ is disfavored statistically by every measure above. It is also, unconditionally,
impossible.

\begin{proposition}[Unboundedness of $e(l)$]
\label{prop:lowerbound}
$e(l) \ge \tfrac14\log_2 l - O(1)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Distinct exponent tuples give distinct sums: since the $a_i$ are distinct, $a_{j-1}$ is the unique
minimal one, so $V=\sum_i 2^{a_i}3^i$ has $2$-adic valuation exactly $a_{j-1}$ (every other term is
divisible by a strictly higher power of $2$), recovering $a_{j-1}$ from $V$ directly. The full term
$2^{a_{j-1}}3^{j-1}$ is then subtracted off, and the process iterates on the remaining $j-1$ terms. So $R_{j-1,j}$, in bijection with $j$-subsets of the
$2j$-element set $\{0,\dots,2j-1\}$, has exact cardinality $\binom{2j}{j}$. Covering
$(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$, of size $2\cdot3^{l-1}$, requires the domain to be at least as large as the
codomain: $\binom{2j}{j} \ge 2\cdot3^{l-1}$ is necessary for $j \ge j^*(l)$. The explicit bound
$\binom{2n}{n} \le 4^n/\sqrt{3n+1}$, valid for every $n\ge1$, turns this into
$4^j \ge 2\cdot3^{l-1}\sqrt{3j+1}$, hence
\[
j \ge l\log_4 3 - \log_4 3 + \log_4 2 + \tfrac12\log_4(3j+1).
\]
Dropping the last, non-negative term already gives $j \ge l\log_4 3 - O(1)$, so
$3j+1 = \Omega(l)$, hence $\log_4(3j+1) \ge \log_4 l - O(1) = \tfrac12\log_2 l - O(1)$. Feeding
this back into the displayed inequality gives the claimed bound.
\end{proof}

\section{A mean-payoff-game upper bound}
\label{sec:mpg}

Reading an admissible tuple's exponents from the bottom up encodes covering as a game of
imperfect information. A state is a unit $z\in\Z_3^*$. A move of cost $d\ge0$ is \emph{legal} at
$z$ when $2^dz\equiv1\pmod3$ (so that $T_d(z):=(2^{d+1}z-2)/3$ is an integer), and \emph{safe} at
window $k$ when, for $m:=3^k$, every one of the three residues $z,z+m,z+2m$ modulo $3m$ maps under
$T_d$ back into a unit modulo $m$. A minimizing player, seeing only $z\bmod3^k$, chooses a legal, safe $d$;
an adversary then reveals the hidden next $3$-adic digit $\epsilon\in\{0,1,2\}$, forcing the true
state to $T_d(z+3^k\epsilon)\bmod3^k$. Both requirements matter: legality alone can send a unit to
$0$ (take $z=1$, $d=0$: $T_0(1)=0$), and the safety filter is exactly what keeps the walk inside
$\Z_3^*$ regardless of which digit the adversary supplies. Legality depends on $d$ only through
$d\bmod2$ (since $2$ has order $2$ modulo $3$), and $2$ is a primitive root modulo $3^{k+1}$ for
every $k\ge0$ (order $2\cdot3^k$, by an elementary lifting-the-exponent induction), so
$2^{d+1}\bmod3^{k+1}$, and hence $T_d(z)\bmod3^k$ and the safety check itself, depend on $d$ only
through $d\bmod(2\cdot3^k)$: successor states repeat with this period as $d$ grows, and every
representative beyond the smallest in its residue class has strictly larger cost for the same
effect, so is never used by an optimal policy. The action set at each state is therefore, without
loss, the subset of $\{0,\dots,2\cdot3^k-1\}$ that is legal and safe there, which is finite. That
this subset is nonempty at every unit state has been verified computationally at every $k=3,\dots,14$
used below, not proven in general here.

This restricted-visibility game is, by construction, a two-player \emph{mean-payoff game} on the
$2\cdot3^{k-1}$ unit states modulo $3^k$, with the now finite legal, safe moves as the minimizer's
edges, the adversary's three choices of $\epsilon$ as the maximizer's edges, and payoff the long-run
average of the move costs $d$. The classical theory of such games, initiated by Ehrenfeucht and
Mycielski~\cite{ehrenfeuchtmycielski1979}, guarantees a value and an optimal positional policy in
general; Theorem~\ref{thm:mpg} below needs none of that generality, only the twelve specific
policies actually exhibited and directly checked in Table~\ref{tab:mpg}.

\begin{empresult}[Soundness of the game]
\label{er:soundness}
At full precision ($k=l$: the true state $z\bmod3^l$ known exactly, no adversary), a play needs only
legality, since $z$ being a unit throughout makes safety automatic: each step chooses $d$ with
$2^dz\equiv1\pmod3$ and moves to $T_d(z)$. For every $l\ge1$ and every unit $z$ modulo $3^l$, a legal
$l$-step play with total cost $J$ starting from $z$ witnesses $z\in R_{J-1,J}\bmod3^l$: reading the
costs $d_0,\dots,d_{J-1}$ from the last step to the first recovers exactly the exponent gaps of an
admissible tuple. Verified against direct brute-force enumeration of $R_{j-1,j}$'s covering sets for
$l=1,\dots,6$, and against the known exact table of $j^*(l)$ (via the full-precision game's
worst-case optimal cost) for $l=1,\dots,12$, with no discrepancy. We do not have a general proof.
\end{empresult}

A window-$k$ policy is a legal full-precision play in this sense too: safety was defined precisely
so that whichever of the three lifts $z,z+3^k,z+2\cdot3^k$ turns out to be the true state modulo
$3^{k+1}$, the move chosen from $z\bmod3^k$ stays legal there, so running a window-$k$ policy against
the actual digit sequence gives a legal full-precision play, and Empirical
Result~\ref{er:soundness} applies to it directly.

\begin{theorem}[Window relaxation bound, conditional on Empirical Result~\ref{er:soundness}]
\label{thm:mpg}
For each $k=3,\dots,14$, the certified policy underlying Table~\ref{tab:mpg} (a legal, safe move at
every unit state modulo $3^k$, average cost $\rho_k$) gives an explicit rational $C_k$ with
$j^*(l) \le \rho_k\cdot l + C_k$ for all $l$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $k$ in range. The policy $\sigma_k$, the value $\rho_k$, and a potential $h_k:(\Z/3^k\Z)^*\to\Q$
are computed by a nested Howard strategy-improvement solver and checked, state by state and digit by
digit over the finite action set established above, against
\[
d_{\sigma_k}(s) + h_k(s'(\epsilon)) \le \rho_k + h_k(s)
\]
at every state $s$ and every adversary digit $\epsilon$, where $s'(\epsilon)$ is the successor under
$\sigma_k$'s chosen move; the matching adversary lower bound extracted from the same run certifies
$\rho_k$ exactly. Summing the inequality along any $l$-step play $s_0,\dots,s_l$ telescopes to
\[
\sum_{i=0}^{l-1} d_{\sigma_k}(s_i) \le l\rho_k + h_k(s_0) - h_k(s_l) \le l\rho_k + \bigl(\max h_k -
\min h_k\bigr) =: l\rho_k + C_k,
\]
regardless of which digits the adversary actually supplies, since the potential inequality holds for
every $\epsilon$ at every step. $\sigma_k$ needs only $z\bmod3^k$ to choose each move, so it is
executable at full precision too, giving a legal $l$-step play at $z$ with total cost at most
$l\rho_k+C_k$; by Empirical Result~\ref{er:soundness}, this bounds the covering budget for $z$
modulo $3^l$. Taking the worst-case $z$ gives $j^*(l) \le \rho_k l + C_k$.
\end{proof}

The gap between this and a fully unconditional theorem is narrow: only the \emph{soundness}
direction is needed (a completed game play constructs a witness), not the harder converse that the
game's value equals $j^*(l)$ exactly.

$\rho_k$ and $C_k$ are computed exactly, as rationals, by a nested Howard strategy-improvement
solver, self-certified at every $k$ via a matching adversary lower bound extracted from the same
run (upper bound equals lower bound, so the computed rational equals $\rho_k$ exactly), and
cross-validated by an independent value-iteration solver and, at $k=4$ and
$k=7$, by running the resulting policy against the actual finite covering DP for every unit residue
modulo $3^l$, $l$ up to $14$, with zero illegal or unsafe moves encountered.
Table~\ref{tab:mpg} gives the values computed for $k=3,\dots,14$.

At $k=14$, $\rho_{14} = 9/8 = 1.125$, giving:

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:mpgbound}
$j^*(l) \le \tfrac98 l + O(1)$.
\end{corollary}

This is, to our knowledge, the best bound on $j^*(l)$'s growth rate currently available, conditional
on Empirical Result~\ref{er:soundness}. $\rho_k$ is observed to be non-increasing over the computed
range $k=3,\dots,14$ (Table~\ref{tab:mpg}); we have not verified this in general, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:mpgbound} uses only the single value $k=14$. The general technique, representing a
covering-type combinatorial problem as a mean-payoff game and computing exact rational relaxation
bounds this way, is not new to this application: beyond Ehrenfeucht and Mycielski's foundational
theorem~\cite{ehrenfeuchtmycielski1979} itself, a close and recent methodological precedent proves
rationality and computability of the covering radius of a primitive sofic shift by essentially the
same construction, applied to a different object~\cite{meyerovitchyoung2026}. We cite both as
precedent for the framing; what is new here is its specific application to $j^*(l)$.

\begin{remark}
Fitting $\rho_k = L + A/k$ by ordinary least squares to $k=10,\dots,14$ gives $L\approx0.876$,
still well above the conjectured true rate $\log_4 3 = 0.7925\ldots$; the fit is sensitive to the
range used (all of $k=3,\dots,14$ gives $L\approx0.904$) and is not evidence of any specific limit.
Only the direction $j^*(l)\le\rho_k l + C_k$, proven above, is established. The reverse inequality,
$\inf_k \rho_k \le \limsup_l j^*(l)/l$, which would be needed to conclude anything about where
$\rho_k$ is heading, is not established, and there is evidence in the course of this work that the
window relaxation may carry an irreducible gap above the true rate.
\end{remark}

\section{The exponential sum, and why it resists a direct attack}
\label{sec:gapA}

Corollary~\ref{cor:mpgbound}'s bound, $1.125\,l$, is well above the conjectured rate
$\log_4(3)\,l \approx 0.7925\,l$. The natural route to closing that gap is Fourier-analytic. By
orthogonality on $\Z/3^l\Z$, the number of tuples landing in residue class $z$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Nz}
N(z) = \frac1{3^l}\sum_{t=0}^{3^l-1} S(t)\,e\!\left(-\frac{tz}{3^l}\right), \qquad
S(t) := \sum_{j+k\ge a_0>\cdots>a_j\ge0} e\!\left(\frac{t}{3^l}\sum_{i=0}^j 2^{a_i}3^i\right),
\end{equation}
and, since $S(0)=|R_{j,k}|=:T$, the triangle inequality gives $N(z)>0$ for every $z$ as soon as
$\sum_{t\ne0}|S(t)| < T$; a uniform bound $|S(t)| = o(T/3^l)$ at every nonzero $t$ would suffice,
at a rate controlled directly by the exponent in the bound. This approach does not work, for three
separate and mutually reinforcing reasons.

\subsection{A hard ceiling on uniform cancellation}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:sqrtceiling}
Any proof of covering via a uniform bound $|S(t)| \le C\sqrt{T}$ for a fixed constant $C$
(square-root cancellation), applied via the triangle inequality across all $3^l-1$ nonzero
frequencies, requires $j \ge 2\log_4(3)\,l - O(\log l) \approx 1.585\,l - O(\log l)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $T := |R_{j-1,j}| = \binom{2j}{j}$. Positivity of $N(z)$ for every $z$ via
$\sum_{t\ne0}|S(t)| < T$ combined with the uniform estimate $|S(t)| \le C\sqrt{T}$ over the
$3^l-1$ nonzero frequencies forces $(3^l-1)C\sqrt{T} < T$, so $T > ((3^l-1)C)^2$, i.e.\
$4^j/\sqrt{3j+1} \ge T > ((3^l-1)C)^2$ (using the explicit bound from Proposition~\ref{prop:lowerbound}'s
proof), which gives $j \ge 2\log_4(3)\,l - O(1)$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:sqrtceiling}'s threshold is \emph{worse} than Corollary~\ref{cor:mpgbound}'s
bound: any approach that only delivers generic square-root cancellation across every
frequency cannot even match what is already known by other means, let alone improve on it. This
rules out the naive version of the Fourier approach cleanly, and directs attention to whether a
finer, frequency-dependent treatment (a major/minor-arc split, in the language of the circle method)
can do better.

\subsection{A computation: the exceptional mass does not stay small}

Numerically, $|S(t)|$ is far from uniform: the frequencies of largest magnitude sit close to dyadic
rationals of small denominator, $t/3^l \approx p/2^r$ for small $r$. The natural repair for
Proposition~\ref{prop:sqrtceiling}'s ceiling is to excise these major-arc resonances and control the
remainder by an averaged bound; whether that remainder stays sparse enough at the scale that
matters is a question this section answers computationally, at $l=18$, with a negative result. A
fixed threshold makes the visible spikes look sparse; the threshold $\eta \sim c/\sqrt T$ that
actually governs the $L^1$ norm is different and gives a different picture: summing every one of
the $8{,}014$ coefficients $S(t)$ with $t$ coprime to $3$ that exceed a generous fixed threshold of
$0.001\cdot T$ contributes less than $132$ to an $L^1$ norm of $5226$, so over $97\%$ of the mass at
this single computed level lives in an exponentially numerous population of near-average-magnitude
coefficients. This is evidence against a sparse-exceptional-set repair at accessible $l$; it says
nothing about every $l$.

The same clustering near dyadic rationals connects the problem to a 2011 discussion of Terence
Tao's, applying Littlewood--Offord and Riesz-product methods to an object built from the same
$2^a3^b$-type exponential sums, in the closely related setting of a hypothetical non-trivial
Collatz cycle~\cite{tao2011blog}; his construction is structurally close to a boundary slice of
$R_{j,k}$ with the bottom exponent pinned at $0$. His own conclusion, in his own words, is that
``once one uses the transcendence theory bound, \dots{} Littlewood-Offord techniques are no longer
of much use,'' and that he knows ``of no way to get a nontrivial separation property between powers
of $2$ and powers of $3$ other than via transcendence theory methods.''

\subsection{A direct test of the residues that actually fail}

The most direct test examines the residues that actually fail to be covered, one budget step
before covering succeeds. At every level checked, their count is tiny (single or low double digits)
against an ambient occupancy mean in the hundreds, sitting in cells whose local intensity, at every
resolution checked, would assign them probability below $e^{-20}$ under a locally homogeneous
model. Holding every $|S(t)|$ fixed while randomizing the phases of conjugate-symmetric pairs
collapses the observed extremal statistics to what a generic-phase model predicts; the actual,
unscrambled phases do not. At the levels checked, coverage failure is decided by phase structure
that magnitude-only methods (uniform bounds, averages, low moments of $S(t)$) do not see.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:1547}
$|S(3^{l-1})|/\binom{2m}{m} \to 1/\sqrt3$ as $m\to\infty$, for the family $R_{m-1,m}$, for every
fixed $l\ge1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$\sum_i 2^{a_i}3^i \equiv 2^{a_0} \pmod 3$, so $S(3^{l-1})/\binom{2m}{m}$ depends only on the
limiting distribution of the top exponent $a_0$'s parity as $m\to\infty$, which converges to the
mixture $\tfrac13\omega + \tfrac23\omega^2$ of the two nontrivial cube roots of unity
$\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$ (the limiting gap $2m-1-a_0$ of the maximum of a random $m$-subset of
$\{0,\dots,2m-1\}$ is geometric with parameter $1/2$, giving $\Pr[a_0\text{ even}]\to1/3$). This
mixture equals $-\tfrac12 - \tfrac{i\sqrt3}6$, of modulus $1/\sqrt3$.
\end{proof}

Uniform decay across every nonzero frequency, the premise a naive statement of the Fourier approach
might suggest, is false at this explicit family of frequencies.

\section{A theorem on the residues that resist coverage}
\label{sec:holdouts}

Write $H(l,j)$ for the set of units modulo $3^l$ \emph{not} covered by $R_{j-1,j}$, so that
$j\ge j^*(l)$ exactly when $H(l,j)=\emptyset$. Every result in this section needs $j\ge l$; at
every computed level $j^*(l) > l$ (Table~\ref{tab:jstar}), but the conjectured rate
$\log_4 3 < 1$ would eventually put $j^*(l)$ below $l$, outside this section's reach. Within this
range, emptiness persists: Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling} below gives $H(l,j)=\emptyset\Rightarrow
H(l,j+1)\subseteq2\emptyset\cap4\emptyset=\emptyset$, so ``$H(l,j)=\emptyset$ exactly when
$j\ge j^*(l)$'' is consistent for every $j\ge l$. We make no claim about $j<l$.

\begin{theorem}[Holdout doubling inclusion]
\label{thm:doubling}
For $j \ge l$, $H(l,j+1) \subseteq 2H(l,j) \cap 4H(l,j)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$R_{j-1,j}$ is the set of values $r=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}2^{b_i}3^i$ over $j$-subsets
$\{b_0>\cdots>b_{j-1}\}\subseteq\{0,\dots,2j-1\}$, and $R_{j,j+1}$ the same with $(j{+}1)$-subsets of
$\{0,\dots,2j+1\}$. Fix $r\in R_{j-1,j}$ with exponent set $S$, and $s\in\{1,2\}$. Shifting every
exponent up by $s$ gives $S+s\subseteq\{s,\dots,2j-1+s\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,2j+1\}$, a $j$-subset
whose induced value is $2^sr$ (a uniform shift of exponents preserves relative rank, so each term's
power of $3$ is unchanged). Since $S+s$ contains no $0$, adjoining $0$ as the new smallest exponent
gives a $(j{+}1)$-subset of $\{0,\dots,2j+1\}$: it becomes the new rank-$j$ term, contributing
$2^0\cdot3^j=3^j$. So this witness represents $2^sr+3^j$, and $3^j\equiv0\pmod{3^l}$ once $j\ge l$.
Hence $2R_{j-1,j}\cup4R_{j-1,j}\subseteq R_{j,j+1}\pmod{3^l}$ for $j\ge l$. So if
$z\in H(l,j+1)$, i.e.\ $z$ is not represented at budget $j+1$, then $z$ lies in neither
$2R_{j-1,j}$ nor $4R_{j-1,j}$: both $z/2$ and $z/4$ are unrepresented at budget $j$, i.e.\
$z\in2H(l,j)\cap4H(l,j)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Chain contraction]
\label{cor:chain}
If $x, 2x, \dots, 2^{t-1}x \in H(l,j+1)$ (all reduced modulo $3^l$, $t\ge1$), then
$x/4, x/2, x, \dots, 2^{t-2}x \in H(l,j)$, a chain of length $t+1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling} to each of the $t$ elements: $2^ix\in H(l,j+1)$ gives
$2^{i-1}x, 2^{i-2}x\in H(l,j)$ for $i=0,\dots,t-1$. The union of these pairs over all $i$ is exactly
$\{x/4,x/2,x,\dots,2^{t-2}x\}$, a doubling chain of length $t+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Run-length bootstrap]
\label{cor:bootstrap}
Writing $\mathrm{maxrun}(H)$ for the greatest $t$ such that $H$ contains $t$ pairwise distinct
elements of the form $x,2x,\dots,2^{t-1}x$ (all reduced modulo $3^l$), with
$\mathrm{maxrun}(\emptyset):=0$, $j^*(l) \le j + \mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))$ for every $j\ge l$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:chain}, $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j)) \ge \mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j+1)) + 1$ whenever
$H(l,j+1)$ is nonempty, so $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))$ strictly increases by at least $1$ as $j$
decreases from $j^*(l)$; equivalently it strictly decreases by at least $1$ at every budget step
increasing from $l$, until it reaches $0$, at which point $H$ is empty.
\end{proof}

\begin{empresult}
\label{er:bootstrap}
Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap} is empirically exact, $j^*(l) = j + \mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,j))$, at every
$(l,j)$ with $l+1\le j\le j^*(l)$ we were able to compute, $l=5,\dots,20$ at every such budget and
$l$ up to $22$ at the single budget $j=l+1$, including a case with $|H(l,j)| > 3\times10^6$. (Past
$j^*(l)$, $H(l,j)=\emptyset$ and $\mathrm{maxrun}(\emptyset)=0$, so the displayed equality no longer
holds there; the claim is only about budgets up to and including $j^*(l)$.)
\end{empresult}

The converse direction, that a doubling chain of the observed maximal length is not merely
sufficient but necessary for survival to that budget, is not proven, and is false as an unrestricted
statement: $1547 \in 2H(7,8)\cap4H(7,8)$, yet $1547 \notin H(7,9)$ (both of $1547$'s two
budget-$9$ witnesses span the full exponent range, the one case Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling}'s
argument does not directly control). We return to a restricted, still-open form of this converse in
Section~\ref{sec:further}.

A natural strengthening of Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling}, that every individual witness admits a
bounded-cost local repair, is also false in its most optimistic form.

Call the \emph{Durfee depth} of a witness $\{a_0>\cdots>a_{j-1}\}\in R_{j-1,j}$ at budget $j$ the
count $\#\{i : a_i \ge j\}$, the number of chosen exponents at or above the budget itself.

\begin{empresult}[Failure of the cost-$1$ local repair rule]
\label{er:falsify}
At the budget transition $(l,j)=(6,10)\to(7,11)$, $100$ of the $1458$ children (residues newly
required for coverage at the larger budget) have minimum achievable Durfee depth exceeding their
parent's minimum depth by more than one, up to three; at $(7,11)\to(8,12)$, $332$ of $4374$
children do, likewise up to three. A correspondingly exhaustive check, measuring witness repair cost
as the minimum number of exponents that must change between a child's cheapest witness and its
cheapest parent witness (matched by the natural containment of exponent sets), finds distances up to
$7$ at both transitions, against a distance of $1$ for the overwhelming majority. This is evidence
against a repair cost of exactly $1$ at these two transitions; it does not establish that no bounded
repair cost exists.
\end{empresult}

This falsifies, in its originally proposed cost-$1$ form, a natural conjecture this project had
earlier entertained (that coverage propagates via a uniform, cost-$1$ local repair rule budget by
budget); its valid surviving content is Theorem~\ref{thm:doubling} and its corollaries above.

\section{Further structure of the last holdouts}
\label{sec:further}

The residues in $H(l,j^*(l)-1)$, the last to be covered, satisfy several further exact laws. For
$W\ge l-1$, write
\[
U(l,W) := \Bigl\{\, \textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{l-1} 2^{\beta_i}3^i \bmod 3^l \ \Bigm|\
W\ge\beta_0>\cdots>\beta_{l-1}\ge0 \,\Bigr\},
\]
an $l$-term analogue of $R_{j-1,j}$ built from a fixed term count and a growing exponent ceiling
$W$.

\begin{lemma}[Width scaling identity]
\label{lem:UW}
For $j\ge l\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:UW}
R_{j-1,j} \equiv 2^{j-l}\,U(l,j+l-1) \pmod{3^l}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $3^i\equiv0\pmod{3^l}$ for $i\ge l$, the residue of $r=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}2^{b_i}3^i\in R_{j-1,j}$
modulo $3^l$ depends only on its top $l$ exponents $b_0>\cdots>b_{l-1}$. A decreasing $l$-tuple
$b_0>\cdots>b_{l-1}\ge0$ with $b_0\le2j-1$ extends to a full admissible $j$-tuple in
$\{0,\dots,2j-1\}$ (appending $j-l$ further, strictly smaller, distinct exponents) exactly when
$b_{l-1}\ge j-l$: append $b_{l-1}-1,\dots,b_{l-1}-(j-l)$ when this holds, and no extension exists
when it does not, since fewer than $j-l$ nonnegative integers lie below $b_{l-1}$. Writing
$c_i := b_i-(j-l)$ turns $b_{l-1}\ge j-l$ into $c_{l-1}\ge0$ and $b_0\le2j-1$ into
$c_0\le2j-1-(j-l)=j+l-1$, and $\sum_i2^{b_i}3^i = 2^{j-l}\sum_i2^{c_i}3^i$. So the top-$l$-exponent
tuples realizable by $R_{j-1,j}$ are exactly $2^{j-l}$ times the decreasing $l$-tuples counted by
$U(l,j+l-1)$, giving the claimed identity modulo $3^l$.
\end{proof}

In particular $j^*(l)$ is the smallest $j\ge l$ with $U(l,j+l-1)=(\Z/3^l\Z)^*$, and writing
$W^*(l):=j^*(l)+l-2$ for the width one step before that,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:HviaK}
H(l,j) = 2^{j-l}\,K(l,j+l-1), \qquad K(l,W) := (\Z/3^l\Z)^* \setminus U(l,W).
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Last-holdout parity]
\label{thm:parity}
If $j^*(l)\ge l+1$, every element of $H(l,j^*(l)-1)$ is $\equiv1\pmod3$. This covers $l=2,\dots,23$
(Table~\ref{tab:jstar}); it fails only at $l=1$ ($j^*(1)=1$, outside \eqref{eq:HviaK}'s range), and
would fail again for any $l$ at which the conjectured rate $\log_43<1$ eventually pushes $j^*(l)$
below $l+1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$U(l,\cdot)$ is monotone increasing in $W$, so $K(l,\cdot)$ is monotone decreasing; every
$x\in K(l,W^*(l))$ is, by minimality of $j^*(l)$, representable at width $W^*(l)+1$ but not at
$W^*(l)$. Any witness for $x$ at width $W^*(l)+1$ using only exponents $\le W^*(l)$ would already
witness $x$ at that smaller width, so every such witness has top exponent
$\beta_0 = W^*(l)+1$. Its contribution to the value, at rank $0$, is $2^{\beta_0}\cdot3^0 =
2^{W^*(l)+1}$, so $x \equiv 2^{W^*(l)+1} \equiv (-1)^{W^*(l)+1} \pmod3$. By \eqref{eq:HviaK}, which
needs $j^*(l)-1\ge l$, elements of $H(l,j^*(l)-1)$ are $2^{j^*(l)-1-l}x$ for such $x$, hence
\[
2^{j^*(l)-1-l}x \equiv (-1)^{j^*(l)-1-l}\cdot(-1)^{W^*(l)+1} = (-1)^{2j^*(l)-2} = 1 \pmod3,
\]
using $W^*(l)+1 = j^*(l)+l-1$ and that $2j^*(l)-2$ is always even.
\end{proof}

\begin{empresult}[Near-extinction bijection]
\label{er:extinction}
$H(l,j^*(l)-1) = 2\cdot\{x \in H(l,j^*(l)-2) : x\equiv2\!\!\pmod3\}$, exactly, verified at every
$l=2,\dots,9$ against the exact table with no exception.
\end{empresult}

The same width mechanism that proves Theorem~\ref{thm:parity} governs this bijection one step
earlier, through the second-highest exponent $\beta_1$ of the extremal witness; unlike the
top-exponent argument above, pinning $\beta_1$ down needs the one-step width recursion
$U(l,W+1)=U(l,W)\cup2U(l,W)\cup\mathrm{Corner}(l,W+1)$ stated below; we omit the full derivation and
record the identity as verified.

An earlier stage of this investigation reported a cross-level ``$\times4$-only'' pattern relating
$H(l+1,j^*(l+1)-1)$ to $H(l,j^*(l)-1)$. Writing $\pi_l$ for reduction modulo $3^l$, the precise
congruence this suggests, $x\equiv4\,\pi_l(x)\pmod{3^{l+1}}$, is impossible whenever $\pi_l(x)$ is a
unit and $l\ge2$: reducing both sides modulo $3^l$ forces $\pi_l(x)\equiv4\,\pi_l(x)\pmod{3^l}$,
i.e.\ $3\,\pi_l(x)\equiv0\pmod{3^l}$. Checked exhaustively at $l=2,\dots,6$, $\pi_l(x)$ never lies in
$H(l,j^*(l)-1)$ for $x\in H(l+1,j^*(l+1)-1)$ in the first place, so the pattern has no witnessing
instance at these levels either. No cross-level relation between $H(l+1,\cdot)$ and $H(l,\cdot)$ is
established by this paper.

\begin{empresult}[Mod-$9$ two-class bound]
\label{er:mod9}
Writing $J:=j^*(l)$, at every $l=3,\dots,9$ checked, $H(l,J-1) \bmod 9 \subseteq \{4^J,
4^{J+1}\}\pmod9$, and the class actually occupied is $4^{J+1}$, never $4^J$, at every level
checked. The two-class containment follows the same mechanism as
Theorem~\ref{thm:parity} one digit further (the mod-$9$ class of a threshold witness is
$2^{\beta_0}+3\cdot2^{\beta_1}\bmod9$, with $\beta_0$ pinned and $\beta_1$'s parity giving the two
options); excluding $4^J$ specifically is the sharpest concrete open question this part of the
investigation produced.
\end{empresult}

\begin{proposition}[Sufficiency of bounded maxrun]
\label{prop:h014}
If $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,l+1))$ is bounded by a constant $C$ for all $l$, then
$j^*(l) \le l + C + 1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap} at $j=l+1$.
\end{proof}

The converse is not established, and the natural argument for it does not work: reading
Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap} downward gives only $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,l+1)) \ge j^*(l)-l-1$, a
\emph{lower} bound on maxrun, which does not force maxrun to stay bounded when $j^*(l)-l$ does.
Proving the converse would need an upper bound on maxrun in terms of $j^*(l)-l$, equivalent to a
tightness statement for Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap} that Section~\ref{sec:discussion}'s
corner-redundancy question addresses and that is false in the strong, per-witness form: recall
$1547$ from Section~\ref{sec:holdouts}.

A naive expectation, that a random set of holdouts at the observed density would have doubling
chains far longer than the flat ceiling of $3$--$4$ measured at every level $l=5,\dots,22$, does not
survive contact with the actual, rapidly declining density of $H(l,l+1)$. An independence model
matched to that actual density tracks the observed sequence within about one unit at every level,
including both the one rise and the one fall in it: $\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,l+1))$ is typical for the
set's density under that model. This does not, by itself, say anything about whether
$\mathrm{maxrun}(H(l,l+1))$ is bounded;
Proposition~\ref{prop:h014} is the only established link between that question and $j^*(l)$'s rate.

The one-step width identity is exact:
\[
U(l,W+1) = U(l,W) \cup 2U(l,W) \cup \mathrm{Corner}(l,W+1),
\]
where $\mathrm{Corner}(l,W+1)$ consists of the witnesses using both the top exponent $W+1$ and the
bottom exponent $0$ simultaneously. At every width $W\ge2l+1$ checked exhaustively, $l=3,\dots,13$,
$\mathrm{Corner}(l,W+1)$ contributes nothing new: every corner witness's value is already covered
without it. If this \emph{corner-redundancy} property holds at every sufficiently large width in
general, it would make $j^*(l)$ computable from a single holdout set, a converse to
Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap} and a real practical gain for extending Table~\ref{tab:jstar} beyond
$l=23$; it remains unproven, with the only known failures at the boundary width $W=2l$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Two named questions summarize what stands between this paper's results and a sharper theorem.
Deciding which of the two classes in Empirical Result~\ref{er:mod9} is excluded would turn the
observed single-class pattern into a proven statement. Proving corner-redundancy at every
sufficiently large width would make Corollary~\ref{cor:bootstrap}'s bootstrap tight in general, not
merely empirically exact at every computed instance, and would settle the converse to
Proposition~\ref{prop:h014} left open above. Neither question is, on its face, as hard as the Weak
Covering Conjecture itself; whether either is substantially easier is not known.

A finite exponential sum built from strictly decreasing, exponentially weighted digit tuples is a
natural object beyond this specific covering problem: finite-population $L$-statistics at non-CLT
frequencies and weights, Littlewood--Offord theory for structured coefficient sequences, and the
analytic side of the Collatz literature via Tao's discussion are all adjacent areas. Three things
found here may recur there under different names: generic cancellation capping out below what
combinatorial counting already proves, exceptional mass that does not concentrate on a sparse set
at the accessible scale, and coverage failure decided by an extremal arithmetic obstruction that a
phase-randomization experiment showed magnitude-only methods miss.

The Weak Covering Conjecture itself, and the exact asymptotic rate of $e(l)$, remain open.
