# H-003: Does Bajnok's sumset/critical-number theory bound j*(l)?

Source: arXiv literature pass (2026-07-27, L-015) surfaced B. Bajnok, "Open Problems About
Sumsets in Finite Abelian Groups: Minimum Sizes and Critical Numbers" (arXiv:1512.03038v2, 15 May
2017; full text fetched and read in full for this note, all 14 pages, not just the abstract).

## Verdict: does not map. Bajnok's theory does not apply to R_{j,k} as a black box.

This is a real negative result, derived below, not a hedge. The reasoning is structural (a
genuine mismatch in what kind of object is being summed), confirmed by an explicit numerical
check against this project's own verified data.

## Bajnok's definitions (verbatim from the paper, Section 1.1)

For a finite abelian group $G$ of order $n$ and an $m$-subset $A = \{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}$ of $G$,
and a nonnegative integer $h$:

- $h$-fold sumset: $hA = \{\sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i a_i \mid \lambda_i \in \mathbb{N}_0,\ \sum \lambda_i = h\}$
  (repetition of elements of $A$ allowed).
- $h$-fold restricted sumset: $\hat{h}A = \{\sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i a_i \mid \lambda_i \in \{0,1\},\ \sum \lambda_i = h\}$
  (exactly $h$ *distinct* elements of $A$, summed with equal weight 1).
- $h$-fold signed sumset: $h_{\pm}A = \{\sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i a_i \mid \lambda_i \in \mathbb{Z},\ \sum|\lambda_i| = h\}$.

The **critical number** $\chi(G,h)$ (and $\hat\chi$, $\chi_\pm$ analogously) is the least $m$ such
that the corresponding sumset equals *all of* $G$, **for every** $m$-subset $A$ of $G$. This is a
worst-case/extremal quantity: it's a statement quantified over all possible generating sets of a
given size, not about one specific set.

The critical property that makes all three of these objects tractable to the theory in this
survey: **the value contributed by a chosen element $a_i$ to the sum does not depend on which
other elements are also chosen, or on any notion of rank/order among the chosen elements.** $A$ is
a genuine subset of the group, and summation in an abelian group is symmetric; $\lambda_i$ only
records "how many times / with what sign was $a_i$ used," never "in what position."

## R_{j,k}'s actual structure (re-derived and numerically verified here)

From the project's verified primary-source definition (`literature/notes/L-001.md`, Wirsching
1998):
$$R_{j,k} := \Big\{ \sum_{i=0}^{j} 2^{a_i} 3^i \ \Big|\ j+k \ge a_0 > a_1 > \cdots > a_j \ge 0 \Big\}$$

and the computation actually used (`experiments/E-001-jstar-fast`) works with $R_{l-1,j}$ via the
reduction $R_{j-1,j} \bmod 3^l = 2^{j-l} \cdot R_{l-1,j} \bmod 3^l$ for $j\ge l$ (a unit multiple,
doesn't change coverage). $R_{l-1,j}$ sums $l$ terms (index $i=0,\ldots,l-1$), with
$(l-1)+j \ge a_0 > a_1 > \cdots > a_{l-1} \ge 0$, i.e. a **strictly decreasing sequence of $l$
distinct values drawn from $\{0,1,\ldots,l+j-1\}$** (a ground set of size $l+j$).

A strictly decreasing sequence of $l$ values is just an $l$-element subset $S \subseteq
\{0,\ldots,l+j-1\}$ written in decreasing order. So: choose an $l$-subset $S$, sort it
$s_1 > s_2 > \cdots > s_l$, and the value is
$$\sum_{i=1}^{l} 2^{s_i}\, 3^{\,i-1}.$$

**This is the crux of the mismatch.** The weight applied to a chosen value $s_i$ is $3^{i-1}$,
where $i$ is $s_i$'s *rank* (position) within the sorted selection $S$ — not a fixed weight tied
to $s_i$ itself, independent of what else is chosen. Change which other elements are in $S$ and
the same value $s$ can end up at a different rank, hence get multiplied by a different power of
3. There is no fixed candidate set $B \subseteq \mathbb{Z}/3^l\mathbb{Z}$ (or any group) for which
$R_{l-1,j} \bmod 3^l$ is expressible as $\hat{l}B$ (or $lB$, or $l_\pm B$) in Bajnok's sense,
because $\hat{h}A$'s defining property is exactly that each element's contribution is
context-independent, and here it provably is not.

### Numerical confirmation

Reproduced the known, verified checkpoint value (`README.md`'s pointwise validation, l=2 j=2:
image = {1,2,5,7} mod 9, missing {4,8}) directly from this subset-plus-rank parametrization:

```python
from itertools import combinations
l, j = 2, 2
vals = set()
for S in combinations(range(l + j), l):
    s = sorted(S, reverse=True)
    v = sum((2**a) * (3**i) for i, a in enumerate(s))
    vals.add(v % (3**l))
print(sorted(vals))   # [1, 2, 5, 7]  -- matches exactly
```

This confirms the subset+rank parametrization is the right restatement of $R_{l-1,j}$ (not just a
plausible-looking guess), which makes the structural argument above concrete rather than
hand-wavy: the object this project actually computes really is rank-weighted, checked against
ground truth, not merely "apparently" rank-weighted from staring at the definition.

## Why the critical-number *framing* doesn't transfer either, independent of the sumset-type mismatch

Even setting aside which of $hA/\hat{h}A/h_\pm A$ (if any) might loosely resemble $R_{l-1,j}$,
Bajnok's critical numbers answer a **universally-quantified worst-case question**: the least $m$
such that *every* $m$-subset's sumset covers $G$. This project's $j^*(l)$ answers a
**existence question about one specific, structured, parametrized family**: does *this
particular* rank-weighted construction, as its size parameter $j$ grows, eventually cover
$(\mathbb{Z}/3^l\mathbb{Z})^*$? These are different questions even when the underlying objects
matched (which they don't here): a critical number bounds the worst case over *all* possible
generating sets of a given size, while $j^*(l)$ is about the behavior of one specific,
non-arbitrary sequence of sets as it grows. A critical-number bound, even if it existed for the
right object, would answer "how many elements does *some* set need in the worst case," not "how
many terms does *this* R_{j,k}-shaped construction need."

## Verdict: blocked, by two independent obstacles, before any real argument gets underway

Checked the specific technical obstacle raised (does the polynomial method work over
$\mathbb{Z}/3^l\mathbb{Z}$, which is not a field for $l \ge 2$) first, since it looked like the
likely single point of failure. It is a real obstacle, and there is a second, independent one on
top of it.

### Obstacle 1: $\mathbb{Z}/3^l\mathbb{Z}$ is not an integral domain for $l\ge2$, and the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz's proof needs "a nonzero degree-$d$ polynomial has at most $d$ roots," which is false there

The Combinatorial Nullstellensatz (Alon 1999) is stated for $F$ a field, precisely because its
proof rests on the elementary fact that a nonzero degree-$d$ univariate polynomial over a field
(or any integral domain) has at most $d$ roots. Verified directly (not taken from a secondary
source) that this fails over $\mathbb{Z}/3^l\mathbb{Z}$ for $l\ge2$, using the simplest possible
test polynomial $f(x)=x^2$ (degree 2):

```
l=1  m=3          roots of x^2 mod m: [0]                                        count=1
l=2  m=9          roots of x^2 mod m: [0, 3, 6]                                  count=3
l=3  m=27         roots of x^2 mod m: [0, 9, 18]                                 count=3
l=4  m=81         roots of x^2 mod m: [0, 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54, 63, 72]         count=9
```

At $l=1$ (where $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{F}_3$ really is a field), the count matches the
field bound exactly (1 root, $\le$ degree 2). At every $l\ge2$ this project actually computes
with, the root count *exceeds* the degree, by a growing margin ($3^{l-1}$ roots for $x^2$
specifically, easy to see directly: $x^2\equiv0\pmod{3^l}$ iff $3^{\lceil l/2\rceil}\mid x$).
This is not a subtle failure mode; it is the textbook consequence of $\mathbb{Z}/3^l\mathbb{Z}$
having zero divisors ($3\cdot3^{l-1}\equiv0$), and it directly breaks the root-counting step the
whole polynomial-method machinery (CN, and the Erdős–Heilbronn/Dias da Silva–Hamidoune proof that
uses CN as its engine) is built on. A short web search for extensions of CN to non-field settings
(Alon's original paper, Pete L. Clark's "The Combinatorial Nullstellensätze Revisited" survey,
and a 2025 extension to skew polynomial rings over *division* rings/algebras) confirms the
standard theorem's hypothesis is a field; the one non-field extension found (skew polynomial
rings) is for division rings, which still have no zero divisors — $\mathbb{Z}/3^l\mathbb{Z}$,
$l\ge2$, is not one. I did not find, in this search, any stated extension of CN or
Erdős–Heilbronn to rings with zero divisors of this specific kind ($\mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$);
absence of a hit in one search session is not proof none exists in the wider literature, and is
flagged as such, not overstated as exhaustive.

**Two ways around obstacle 1, both checked and both dead ends for this problem specifically**:

- *Reduce to $\mathbb{F}_3$*: valid application domain for CN, but the covering question this
  project asks is specifically about resolution mod $3^l$ for growing $l$; collapsing to mod 3
  throws away exactly the fine structure $j^*(l)$ is about. A bound obtained this way would say
  something about coverage mod 3 (trivial: 3 residues), not mod $3^l$.
- *Lift to $\mathbb{Z}_3$, the 3-adic integers*: $\mathbb{Z}_3$ is an integral domain (no zero
  divisors: the root-counting lemma *does* hold there), but (a) it is infinite, so "covering all
  of a finite group" has to be reformulated as a density/measure statement to even make sense,
  and (b) that reformulation is exactly Tao's beta=1 / density-of-Collatz-preimages framing this
  project already has as H-002, not a new angle opened up by this attempt.

### Obstacle 2: independent of the ring, $R_{j,k}$'s rank-dependent weighting is not a symmetric function of the chosen set, which the polynomial-method sumset machinery requires

This is the same structural fact notes/H-003.md's first section already established for
Bajnok's theorems (rank-in-selection determines the weight $3^{\text{rank}}$, not a fixed
per-element weight), but it independently blocks the polynomial-method route too, for a reason
specific to how that method is built, not just imported from the Bajnok section.

The standard Erdős–Heilbronn-via-Combinatorial-Nullstellensatz proof works with a polynomial
$P(x_1,\ldots,x_h) = \prod_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)\cdot Q(x_1+\cdots+x_h)$ (schematically): the Vandermonde
factor enforces *distinctness* of the chosen values, and the rest of the construction is a
function of the *sum* $x_1+\cdots+x_h$, i.e. symmetric in the $x_i$: relabeling which variable
holds which value doesn't change $x_1+\cdots+x_h$. This symmetry is what lets the argument treat
the $x_i$ as unordered set elements at all.

$R_{l-1,j}$'s value, $\sum_{i=1}^{l}2^{s_i}3^{i-1}$ with $s_1>\cdots>s_l}$, is a function of the
*set* $\{s_1,\ldots,s_l\}$ (the rank of each element is determined once the set is fixed, not an
extra free choice), but it is not a function of the form $\sum_i g(s_i)$ for any fixed $g$
applied independently to each element: which power of 3 a given value $s$ contributes depends on
how many of the *other* chosen values exceed it. Relabeling/permuting which "slot" holds which
value changes the sum, unlike a genuine sumset. There is no way to write this as
$Q(x_1+\cdots+x_l)$ for a symmetric $Q$; the natural polynomial encoding of $R_{l-1,j}$'s value
would need $\sum_i 2^{x_i}\,3^{\#\{k : x_k > x_i\}}$, and the exponent $\#\{k:x_k>x_i\}$ is
itself a non-polynomial, order-dependent function of the *other* variables — not something
Combinatorial Nullstellensatz's machinery (which reasons about coefficients of an honest
multivariate polynomial) is set up to touch, independent of which ring the $x_i$ live in.

### A reframing that might be a real, unexplored path (flagged for a second opinion, not pursued further here)

One structural observation worth recording rather than discarding: the ordering constraint
$a_0>a_1>\cdots>a_l$ that defines $R_{j,k}$ lives on the *exponent/index domain*
$\{0,\ldots,l+j-1\}\subset\mathbb{Z}$, which is genuinely ordered (it's a set of integers), before
any reduction mod $3^l$ happens. The obstruction above is about applying the polynomial method
*in the target ring* $\mathbb{Z}/3^l\mathbb{Z}$; it says nothing about whether a polynomial-method
argument could work *on the source side*, treating the choice of an $l$-subset of
$\{0,\ldots,l+j-1\}$ (an honest subset-choice problem over $\mathbb{Z}$, or over a large enough
prime field via reduction, where CN applies cleanly) and only mapping to
$\mathbb{Z}/3^l\mathbb{Z}$ at the very end via the fixed evaluation map
$S\mapsto\sum_{s\in S}2^s3^{\mathrm{rank}(s)}$. This would be a genuinely new construction, not an
application of any existing theorem found in this pass, and I have not attempted it (would
require inventing the right auxiliary polynomial, not looking one up) — flagging it as the one
concrete idea from this pass that seems worth a second opinion before deciding whether to invest
real effort, per the instruction not to let this session's own confidence be the final word on a
genuine open research question.

## Verdict: the reframing does not add real power, because it turns out to be the *same object* as the original definition, not a genuinely different one

Following the instruction to first state the construction precisely enough to attempt a proof,
before attempting anything: what exactly is "the ordered exponent domain"?

Re-reading Wirsching's actual definition (`notes/H-001.md`, `literature/notes/L-001.md`), $R_{j,k}$
was never defined by "choose an unordered subset, then sort it and read off ranks." It is defined
directly as a sum over an **explicit descending chain**:
$$R_{j,k} := \Big\{ \textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{j} 2^{a_i}3^i \ \Big|\ j{+}k \ge a_0 > a_1 > \cdots > a_j
\ge 0 \Big\}.$$
The "choose a set $S$, sort it, and let the value be $\sum 2^{s_i}3^{\mathrm{rank}(s_i)}$"
formulation used in the first (Bajnok) section of this note is a *faithful restatement* of this
same object (confirmed there numerically), not a different, more constrained special case of it.
There is no separate "unordered" version of $R_{j,k}$ that a rank/sorting operation is applied to;
the ordering constraint $a_0>a_1>\cdots>a_j$ **is** the original definition, present from the
start, not introduced as an artifact of translating into Bajnok's sumset language.

Numerically re-confirmed this is the same object either way it's parametrized (direct descending
chain vs. subset-then-sort), reproducing the same known checkpoint value:
```python
from itertools import combinations
l, j = 2, 2
vals = {sum((2**a[i])*(3**i) for i in range(l))
        for a in (sorted(c, reverse=True) for c in combinations(range(l+j), l))}
print(sorted(v % 3**l for v in vals))   # [1, 2, 5, 7] -- matches, as expected (same object)
```

**Why this sinks the specific reframing being tested**: the Combinatorial-Nullstellensatz /
Erdős–Heilbronn machinery's actual value-add is *breaking symmetry* on an otherwise unordered,
symmetric sumset — the Vandermonde factor $\prod_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)$ exists specifically to force
*distinctness* among variables that would otherwise be freely interchangeable, which is what lets
the argument treat "which values got chosen" as the only thing that matters (order-independent).
$R_{j,k}$'s domain has no such symmetry to begin with: it is *already* an explicitly ordered chain
by definition, with no relabeling freedom (the constraint isn't merely $a_i$ pairwise distinct, it
is a specific total order, and each position's exponent-of-3 weight is fixed by that position, not
discovered after the fact by sorting). There is no symmetric object here for a Vandermonde-style
device to act on; the "reframing" just re-derives the same explicit map
$f(a_0,\ldots,a_{l-1}) = \sum_i 2^{a_i}3^i \bmod 3^l$ on the same explicit domain
$\{(a_0,\ldots,a_{l-1}) : l{+}j{-}1\ge a_0>\cdots>a_{l-1}\ge0\}$ that the project already computes
directly. Working "on the source side" doesn't introduce a new polynomial-method entry point,
because there was never an unordered/symmetric structure on the source side to exploit in the
first place; $j^*(l)$ is, and always was, a direct question about one explicit, already-ordered
map's image, not a sumset needing symmetry-breaking to become tractable.

A secondary, independent reason CN-style tools don't gain traction even granting the reframing:
CN's coefficient-extraction step is a *field*-polynomial argument, and $f$'s term $2^{a_i}$ is
exponential, not polynomial, in the variable $a_i$; making that rigorous inside CN's framework
would require its own nontrivial construction that was not attempted here (a genuine, separate,
unresolved technical gap, flagged rather than glossed over) — but the point above (no symmetry to
break) already makes the reframing not useful even if this second issue were somehow solved.

## A genuinely different, more promising lead surfaced by the required prior-art search (not pursued, flagged only)

Searching for prior art on "weighted/positional subset sums, prime-power modulus" (as instructed)
surfaced a different technique family that looks like a much better fit than the polynomial method,
worth recording even though not pursued here: **character sums / Ramanujan sums**, the classical
analytic-number-theory tool for exact or asymptotic subset-sum counting in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$
for *any* $n$, prime or composite. Concretely: J. Li and D. Wan, "Counting subset sums of finite
abelian groups" (fetched and read the introduction and Theorem 1.1/Corollary 1.2 directly,

modulus $n$ (prime or prime-power, no field requirement — Ramanujan/Gauss sums are defined via
roots of unity, not via the root-counting lemma that sank both prior attempts). This is exactly
the kind of "does a structured sum hit every residue mod $3^l$" machinery that's native to
composite moduli, unlike Combinatorial Nullstellensatz.

**This is not a ready-made answer**: Li–Wan's theorem is for *plain, unweighted* $k$-subset sums

weight $3^{\mathrm{rank}}$) that blocked both prior approaches would need to be addressed before

sums over $\mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$ as a matter of course (this is their entire domain of
applicability in analytic number theory), which is precisely where both previous attempts failed
structurally. Adapting a character-sum/exponential-sum argument to $R_{j,k}$'s actual rank-weighted
structure is a real, unexplored, and plausible-looking direction — genuinely new research, not a

### What Li–Wan's paper actually proves (read directly, `https://www.math.uci.edu/~dwan/liwan4.pdf`, fetched, converted with `pdftotext -layout`, and read section 1 in full)

J. Li and D. Wan, "Counting Subset Sums of Finite Abelian Groups." For $D\subseteq A$ a subset of
size $n$ of a finite abelian group $A$, $N_D(k,b)$ counts $k$-element subsets $S\subseteq D$ with
$\sum_{a\in S}a=b$ (a **plain, unweighted, order-independent** group sum). Their Theorem 1.1 gives
an exact formula for $N(k,b)$ when $D=A$ (the whole group), via
$$N(k,b)=\frac1n\sum_{r\mid(n,k)}(-1)^{k+k/r}\binom{n/r}{k/r}\Phi(r,b),\qquad
\Phi(r,b)=\sum_{d\mid r,\,(n_i,d)\mid b_i}\mu(r/d)\prod_i(n_i,d),$$
specializing for cyclic $A=\mathbb Z_n$ (Corollary 1.2) to a formula in terms of Ramanujan's sum
$C_r(b)=\sum_{d\mid(r,b)}\mu(r/d)\,d$. The derivation (Section 2, skimmed for method, not
reproduced in full here) uses the standard fact that a group character turns a sum into a
product, $\chi(a_1+\cdots+a_k)=\chi(a_1)\cdots\chi(a_k)$, then exploits that **every element of
the full group $A$ plays a symmetric role**, so the resulting character sum $\sum_{a\in A}\chi(a)$
collapses to a simple closed form (this is exactly Ramanujan's sum, whose Möbius-function formula
is classical) independent of which specific elements were "chosen."

### Setting up the analogous decomposition for $R_{l-1,j}$: valid, verified numerically

$N(c)$, the number of admissible tuples $(a_0>\cdots>a_{l-1})$ (from $\{0,\ldots,l+j-1\}$) with
$\sum_i 2^{a_i}3^i\equiv c\pmod{3^l}$, admits the same Fourier/character decomposition as any
function into $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ ($n=3^l$), by definition of the discrete Fourier transform,
regardless of any structure in how the tuples are chosen:
$$N(c)=\frac1n\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}e^{-2\pi i tc/n}\,S(t),\qquad
S(t)=\!\!\sum_{a_0>\cdots>a_{l-1}}\!\! e^{2\pi i t\left(\sum_i 2^{a_i}3^i\right)/n}.$$
Verified this numerically against the known checkpoint (l=2, j=2, direct enumeration gives
$N(0..8)=[0,1,2,0,0,2,0,1,0]$, matching {1,2,5,7} each once except 2 and 5 twice, missing 4 zeros
elsewhere — consistent with the known image $\{1,2,5,7\}$): computed $S(t)$ directly for all
$t=0,\ldots,8$ and inverted; the Fourier-recovered $N(c)$ matched the direct count exactly
(`[-0.0, 1.0, 2.0, -0.0, 0.0, 2.0, 0.0, 1.0, -0.0]` vs. direct `[0,1,2,0,0,2,0,1,0]`, differing
only by floating-point rounding of exact zeros). **This is a genuinely different outcome from the
first two attempts**: those failed to even produce a valid symmetric object for their respective
machinery to act on; this decomposition is unconditionally valid (it's just Fourier inversion),
and the numerical check confirms the $S(t)$ definition used is the correct one for this problem,
not a mis-transcription.

### Where it stops working: Li–Wan's closed form for $S(t)$-type sums needs element-genericity that $R_{j,k}$'s terms don't have

Character-turns-sum-into-product still holds here too:
$\chi(\sum_i 2^{a_i}3^i)=\prod_i\chi(2^{a_i}3^i)$, since this is a group-theoretic fact about any
additive decomposition, not specific to unweighted subset sums. The obstruction is not at this
step. It is in the *next* step, where Li–Wan use the fact that $D=A$ (or a generic subset) lets
them evaluate $\sum_{\text{choices}}\prod_i\chi(a_i)$ combinatorially, symmetric-function style,
because every element of the group is interchangeable with every other as far as the counting
argument is concerned. Here, the "elements" being summed are $2^{a_i}3^i$ for $a_i$ ranging over a
*shrinking, order-coupled* domain (each $a_i$ must be strictly less than the previous one, and the
weight $3^i$ is tied to *which pick number* $i$ this is, not to $a_i$ itself) — the same
rank-dependence that blocked both prior attempts, restated here in the character-sum setting: there
is no known reduction of $S(t)$ to a Möbius/Ramanujan-sum closed form for this kind of
position-coupled, geometrically-structured ($2^{a_i}$, exponential in $a_i$) element family. Li–Wan's
paper, read in full for Section 1, contains no treatment of order-dependent or per-position-weighted
sums; every construction there is over an literal, unstructured subset of group elements.

### Trying to use the valid decomposition anyway: no shortcut, same cost as the existing algorithm

Since $S(t)$ has no known closed form here, the natural fallback is computing it directly. $S(t)$
admits an exact recursion by processing candidate values $a=l+j-1,\ldots,0$ from high to low and
tracking how many have been "taken" so far (the next taken value gets assigned the current count
as its rank $i$, exactly matching $R_{j,k}$'s definition): this is *structurally the same
transfer-process* as the bitset+rotation DP `experiments/E-001-jstar-fast/src/main.rs` already
implements, just tracking a single complex accumulator per residue class weighted by $t$ instead
of one bit per residue tracked simultaneously for all $t$ at once. Computing every $S(t)$ for
$t=0,\ldots,n-1$ this way costs at least as much as the existing DP (which computes the full
image in one pass, all residues simultaneously, via bitwise operations) — there is no
computational or theoretical shortcut available through this route without an actual closed-form
evaluation of $S(t)$, which was not found.

### One structural fact surfaced, flagged but not pursued further

Checked (numerically, for $l=1..7$, and this generalizes by the standard lifting-the-exponent
argument since $\mathrm{ord}_3(2)=2$ and $2\not\equiv1\pmod9$): $\mathrm{ord}_{3^l}(2)=2\cdot3^{l-1}=\varphi(3^l)$
for every $l$ tested, i.e. **2 is a primitive root modulo $3^l$** for all $l$. This means
$\{2^a\bmod 3^l\}$ ranges over the *entire* unit group $(\mathbb Z/3^l\mathbb Z)^*$ as $a$ varies,
with a known, structured period. Whether this specific fact (2 being a primitive root, not just
some element) can be leveraged to actually evaluate or bound $S(t)$ — turning it into a genuine
Gauss-sum-like object over the cyclic group generated by 2 — is a real, concrete, more specific

## What was found: a real, structurally close match, with the same limitation as every prior round

**T. Cochrane's exponential-sums-modulo-prime-powers research program** (L-019, L-020 in
`literature/INDEX.md`) develops exactly the relevant classical machinery: for a fixed primitive
root $a$ mod $p^m$, the $p$-adic logarithm converts the multiplicative action of a character on
$a^k$ into a **linear function of $k$** (L-019 eq. 1.4-1.5: $\chi(a^k) = e\big(2\pi i\,ck /
(p^{m-1}(p-1))\big)$). Substituting $p=3$, $a=2$ (our primitive root, verified in round 4) lines
up structurally with $H$-003's setting about as closely as anything found across six framework
rounds and this search.

**The limitation, found immediately and not glossed over**: every sum Cochrane's method treats is
a **complete sum over the full residue range** $x=1,\ldots,p^m$ (or a full coset structure derived
from it). $S(\xi) = \sum_{a_0>a_1>\cdots>a_{l-1}} e(2\pi i\,\xi\sum_k 2^{a_k}3^k/3^l)$ sums over a
strictly-decreasing-sequence-restricted domain, a small, rank-coupled proper subset of all
possible exponent tuples, not a complete range. This is the exact same restricted-domain
obstruction that blocked Bajnok (round 1), the polynomial method (rounds 2-3), and Li-Wan (round
4). Cochrane's linearization trick for the primitive-root power is real and directly relevant, but
it addresses a different part of the problem than the part that has actually been blocking every
attempt; it does not by itself resolve the restricted-domain issue.
